This is not a summary. A summary tells you what was said. This document rehearses what you will be asked to do in the final examination, which is a different activity, and the difference is the whole point of a course named Assessment rather than Introduction.

Every question below carries four things: the chapter and sub-chapter it comes from, a hint you may read before answering, the answer, and a short explanation of why that answer is defensible and the others are not. Read the hint before you look at the options. If the hint alone is enough for you to produce the answer, you know that sub-chapter. If it is not, go back to the sub-chapter named in the Covers line and re-read that section only. That is the fastest revision route available to you, and it is why the Covers line exists on every single item.

The course has argued the same thing for four weeks in four different vocabularies. Here it is one final time, because the examiner is marking for it.

A claim without a named measurement is an opinion, and an opinion scores nothing.

Two students describe the same system. One writes that the new portal "improved things a lot and users are happy." The other writes that "median time from request to confirmed booking fell from 31 hours to 4 minutes across the eight weeks either side of launch, and no satisfaction survey has been run, so the claim about user preference is unsupported and has been removed from this report."

The second student wrote fewer adjectives and earned more marks. Notice what the second sentence does that the first cannot: it invites a sceptic to go and check, and it openly declares one thing the writer does not know. Both properties are worth marks. In this course, admitting an unknown scores higher than concealing it, because a concealed unknown is the failure mode that ends careers.

Carry this into every essay and every case study below.

Every organisation, standard or regulator named in this review is linked to its own official site, with the address written out in full so that the link remains usable if you print this file or read it offline. Links point only to primary sources — the body that publishes the thing being discussed — never to a summary of it. Where a topic is legal, the link goes to the regulator, not to a law firm's commentary.

Three of the four options are wrong for a stateable reason. Find the reason. In this course the wrong options are usually one of four kinds: a definition from an adjacent concept (offering throughput when the question is about bandwidth), a true statement that does not answer the question asked, a claim that is right in general but wrong for the level of management named in the stem, or the answer a student gives who learned the vocabulary but not the distinction.

Read the stem twice before reading any option. Most MCQ errors in this subject are stem-reading errors, not knowledge errors.

The bracket next to each question tells you how many answers are expected. This is a gift and you should treat it as one: it converts guessing into arithmetic. If the bracket says three and you are certain of two, you are choosing one option from the remaining four, not one from six.

Never select more than the stated number hoping to catch the right ones. Over-selection is marked as error in this course, and a student who selects five when three were asked has told the examiner that they cannot distinguish the concept from its neighbours.

Four to eight sentences. A short essay in this course has a fixed skeleton and you should use it every time:

  1. Name the concept in the vocabulary of the course.
  2. Define it in one sentence that does not use the word being defined.
  3. Apply it to the situation in the question, naming a specific actor and a specific mechanism.
  4. State the measurement that would show whether your claim is true.
  5. Name one limitation of your own answer.

Step five is the one students skip and the one that separates a mark in the seventies from a mark in the nineties. An analyst who cannot name the weakness in their own recommendation has not finished the analysis.

Use the six-lens method from Week 01, Lesson 1.5, unless the question tells you otherwise. Purpose, People, Process, Strategy, Ethics, Evidence — in that order, one paragraph each, one measurement per claim. Score the weakest lens, never the average, because averaging hides exactly the defect you were asked to find.

§1.1.1 — Data, information, knowledge. Three words, three different things, and systems are built differently depending on which one they are meant to produce. Data are recorded facts with no interpretation attached — a date, a job number, a quantity. Information is data arranged so that it answers a question somebody actually asked. Knowledge explains why the information looks the way it does and what to do about it, and it normally lives in a person rather than in a database. The examinable consequence: when a system is praised for "providing good information", the professional first question is which question does it answer, and who asked it? Reports nobody requested are data with formatting.

§1.1.2 — What an information system is. A set of related components that collect raw facts, transform them, and deliver a result to people who act on it, together with the feedback that corrects the collection next time. The word computer is deliberately absent. Four activities:

ActivityWhat happensFailure mode
InputFacts captured from inside or outside the organisationWrong data enters; everything downstream inherits the error
ProcessingFacts sorted, calculated, compared, validated, combinedArithmetic correct, rule out of date, answer wrong
OutputResult reaches a person in an actionable formRight answer, wrong time, wrong format, wrong person
FeedbackResult changes the input next timeLoop never closed; the same flawed report runs for years

Feedback is the activity beginners omit, and it is the one that turns data processing into a system. Wrapped around the four sit the organisation (departments, titles, rules, habits) and the environment (customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, shareholders), which supplies much of the input and changes without permission.

§1.1.3 — The three dimensions. Every system is simultaneously organisation, management and technology. When a system fails, the cause is normally in the dimension nobody was watching — and it is rarely technology, because technology fails loudly and gets fixed, while organisational and management defects fail quietly and persist. Describing a system only in technical terms is one third of an answer and is marked as such.

§1.1.4 — The six strategic business objectives. Organisations buy systems for one or more of exactly six reasons: operational excellence; new products, services and business models; customer and supplier intimacy; improved decision making; competitive advantage; and survival. Two claimed precisely beats six claimed vaguely, every time. Survival is the objective students under-use: a clinic adopting electronic records because the regulator no longer accepts paper gains no advantage — every clinic must do it — but the alternative is closure.

§1.1.5 — Complementary assets. The additional investments required before a technology investment produces any value. Organisational (supportive culture, an efficient process to run the system inside, decision authority near the information), managerial (senior backing, incentives that reward using the system rather than working around it, training, knowledge sharing), and social (telecommunications infrastructure, a labour market with the skills, standards, enforceable law). The practical consequence is the sentence to memorise: the software is rarely the expensive part and almost never the part that fails.

§1.1.6 — Four system types. TPS (Transaction Processing System) for front-line staff — "record this sale." MIS (Management Information System) for middle managers — "how did we do against plan?" DSS (Decision Support System) for analysts — "what happens if we change this?" ESS (Executive Support System) for senior management — "where is this organisation going?" Three points carry the marks: data flows upward, so a corrupt TPS produces confident wrong answers at every level above it; MIS means two things (the reporting layer, and the name of this whole academic field); and these are not products — no vendor sells "a DSS", they are a classification of purpose.

§1.1.7 — Three ways of studying the field. The technical approach optimises the system as a formal object. The behavioural approach studies what the system does to people's behaviour and power. The sociotechnical view — the one this course uses — holds that technology and organisation adjust to each other and that the best result normally requires changing both. The working rule: when a system fails, look for the human decision that made the technical failure possible.

§1.2.1 — Business processes. A specific sequence of steps, performed by specific people, producing something a customer or another department needs. Customers experience processes; organisations describe themselves by departments; the two do not match. The four you must be able to name: order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, hire-to-retire, record-to-report. The critical sentence: the crossings between departments are where information is lost, delayed, duplicated or contradicted, and most of the enterprise software industry exists to address that one fact.

§1.2.2 — Functional systems and silos. Sales, manufacturing, finance and human resources each historically bought their own system at a different time from a different vendor. Each works. What fails is what happens between them. Information silos are not caused by bad staff or bad software — they are caused by each function optimising its own work without a shared record.

§1.2.3 — The four enterprise applications. ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) — one integrated system on one database across most core functions. SCM (Supply Chain Management) — coordinates suppliers, production and distribution, and extends the system beyond the firm's walls, which means depending on data quality you do not control. CRM (Customer Relationship Management) — stores every customer interaction so the relationship becomes an organisational asset rather than one salesperson's memory. KMS (Knowledge Management System) — captures expertise so it survives a departure; the hardest of the four, because it depends on busy people writing down what they know in time nobody pays for. The ERP trade-off to state in any assessment: it removes the walls between departments and it removes their independence, because every department must now accept one common definition of "customer", "order" and "revenue".

§1.2.4 — Intranets and extranets. An intranet is private web technology restricted to insiders. An extranet is the same deliberately extended to named outsiders, usually suppliers or major customers. The interesting question is not technical: what has a business partner now become dependent on, and what happens to them if you switch it off? That dependency is a switching cost, and creating it deliberately is a recognised strategy (see §1.3.5).

§1.2.5 — E-business, e-commerce, e-government. E-business is digital technology applied to any organisational process, internal or external — a factory scheduling system qualifies, and no selling is involved. E-commerce is the subset involving buying and selling over a network, divided into B2C (Business to Consumer), B2B (Business to Business) and C2C (Consumer to Consumer); by transaction value B2B is by far the largest, which surprises students because B2C is the part they personally see. E-government is the same technology used by public bodies. Two properties drive the economics: marginal cost near zero for digital goods, and network usefulness that grows with each additional participant.

§1.2.6 — Collaboration. Working with others toward a shared, explicit goal. It has grown in importance because work moved from routine to interpretive, organisations flattened, firms distributed geographically, and innovation is now understood as a group process. Tools sort by when and where: same time/same place, same time/different place, different time/same place, different time/different place. The different time, different place quadrant is the hardest and the most valuable, because it is the only one that scales across time zones without exhausting people. The measurement trap: count decision cycle time, not message volume. Message volume always rises; that is not evidence of anything.

§1.2.7 — Who runs the function. CIO (Chief Information Officer) — the whole function and its alignment with business goals. CSO (Chief Security Officer) — protection of information and physical assets. CPO (Chief Privacy Officer) — lawful and fair handling of personal data. CKO (Chief Knowledge Officer) — capture and sharing of organisational knowledge. CDO (Chief Data Officer) — how data is collected, governed and used. Below them: systems analysts, who translate business problems into system requirements and perform the single most important translation in the field; programmers; network specialists; database administrators; and end users, the largest group and the largest source of both requirements and unofficial workarounds. "The IT department" is not a name, and an assessment that fails to name an accountable role has not finished.

§1.3.1 — Two definitions of an organisation. The technical definition: a stable formal structure taking resources from the environment and processing them into outputs. The behavioural definition: a collection of rights, privileges, obligations and responsibilities balanced over time through conflict and negotiation. You need both, because the technical definition tells you what a system could change and the behavioural definition tells you what it will be permitted to change.

§1.3.2 — Features every organisation has. Routines (standard operating procedures) make an organisation efficient at what it repeats and clumsy at anything new. Organisational politics are not a character defect — different positions are accountable for different things, so a finance director resisting a system that reduces financial control is doing their job. Culture is the set of assumptions nobody articulates, simultaneously a stabiliser and a brake. Environments change faster than organisations, which is the main reason organisations fail; note that organisations also shape their environments through lobbying, standards and relationships.

§1.3.3 — Two economic explanations. Transaction cost theory: firms exist because buying in a market costs money — search, negotiation, contracts, verification. Systems lower those costs, making outside purchase cheaper relative to internal production, so firms can stay smaller while handling more business. Agency theory: owners hire agents with their own interests, so supervision costs money; systems reduce supervision cost, so one manager oversees more people and layers disappear. Both predict smaller, flatter organisations for different reasons, and naming which mechanism is operating in a given case is worth marks.

§1.3.4 — Resistance to change. A system changes four things at once — task, technology, people, structure — and all four are interlocked. When a system is described as having "failed", ask which of the four changed and which did not. A system that changed the technology and left the structure untouched has not failed technically; it has been rejected organisationally, and more software will not fix it.

§1.3.5 — The five competitive forces. Published by Michael E. Porter of Harvard Business School in 1979; official source, the Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness, Harvard Business School — https://www.isc.hbs.edu.

Read the analysis this way: a strong force means value is leaving the firm through that channel. The framework tells you where the money is going. It does not tell you what to do — that is the next step, and it is yours.

§1.3.6 — Four generic strategies. Low-cost leadership, product differentiation, focus on a market niche, and strengthening customer and supplier intimacy. Most successful firms pursue one primarily and borrow from a second. The caveat most textbooks skip and this course examines: buying a system does not produce a strategy, because every competitor can buy the same licence within a quarter. Advantage comes from the complementary assets of §1.1.5.

§1.3.7 — Value chain and value web. The value chain splits a firm into primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, sales and marketing, service) and support activities (firm infrastructure, human resources, technology development, procurement). Its analytical use is to convert an unanswerable question — "where should we apply technology?" — into an answerable one: "which single activity, if improved, would most change what the customer experiences or what the firm spends?" Benchmarking against an industry standard tells you whether an activity is genuinely weak or merely unfamiliar. The value web extends the idea across firms, with the strategic consequence that competitive position now depends partly on systems you neither own nor control.

§1.3.8 — Network economics, ecosystems, disruption. Network economics: each additional participant makes the network more valuable to every existing participant at near-zero cost, which pushes platform markets toward a few large winners. Business ecosystems: loosely coupled firms around a keystone supplying a platform, with niche firms building on it — and dependence on the keystone is a risk that appears on no balance sheet. Disruptive technologies arrive worse, serve a market incumbents do not want, and improve until they displace them; the incumbent's failure is usually rational at every individual step, which is why "the managers were stupid" is both wrong and unassessable. First-mover advantage is not automatic — it lasts only where a network effect, switching cost, patent or accumulated data asset makes the position defensible.

§1.4.1 — Ethics is not law. Ethics is the set of principles used to choose between available courses of action for which you are responsible. Something can be legal and wrong, or illegal and defensible. The reason ethical questions crowd this field is unglamorous: information systems make new actions cheap enough to be worth doing. Tracking where every student sits was always possible with a clipboard; it was not worth doing. Sensors made it nearly free, and things that are nearly free get done without anyone deciding. Hence the exam-relevant reframing: the ethical question is rarely "should we do this obviously bad thing?" It is "who decided this, when, and did they know they were deciding it?" — a question with a findable answer.

§1.4.2 — The five moral dimensions. Information rights and obligations (what may be collected, what must be disclosed, what claims a person retains — including privacy and informational self-determination); property rights and obligations; accountability, liability and control; system quality; quality of life.

§1.4.3 — Five trends that raise the stakes. Rising computing power (analyse everything rather than sample); falling storage cost (keep forever, so "delete when no longer needed" stops being the default); advances in data analysis (profiling — building a detailed picture of a person from many individually harmless records); networking advances (data crosses jurisdictions unnoticed); mobile devices (tracking becomes the default rather than a decision). Non-obvious relationship awareness finds connections between people that neither declared — legitimately valuable in fraud detection and legitimately dangerous elsewhere, using the identical technique.

§1.4.4 — Responsibility, accountability, liability. Responsibility — you accept the costs and duties of a decision; a person takes it on. Accountability — a mechanism exists to determine who took an action; it is a property of a system, not of a person, and where no mechanism exists nobody is accountable no matter who feels responsible. Liability — a legal framework permits recovering damages. The failure this course cares about is the accountability gap: a system acts, someone is harmed, and no mechanism can identify which human decision produced it. "The algorithm did it" describes an accountability gap; it does not explain one. Course rule: every automatic decision must have a named human who can reverse it.

§1.4.5 — The five-step ethical analysis. (1) Identify and describe the facts. (2) Define the conflict and name the higher-order values — ethical questions are usually one legitimate value against another, not good against bad. (3) Identify the stakeholders — the step most often skipped and the one that most changes the answer, because it forces you to name somebody not in the room. (4) Identify the options you can reasonably take — there are almost never two; usually four or five, and the good one is usually the third. (5) Identify the consequences of each option, for each stakeholder, not only for the organisation.

§1.4.6 — Six candidate principles. The Golden Rule; Kant's Categorical Imperative (if it is not right for everyone, it is not right for anyone); the Slippery Slope Rule (if it cannot be repeated indefinitely without harm, do not start); the Utilitarian Principle (greatest value for the greatest number — with its known weakness, that it can justify harm to a minority); the Risk Aversion Principle (prefer the option whose worst case is survivable); the No Free Lunch Rule (assume what someone else created is theirs unless declared otherwise — absence of a copyright notice is not permission). In written work, choose two that point in different directions, apply both, and say which you followed and why. An analysis where all six agree is an analysis of an easy case.

§1.4.7 — Professional codes. ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) — https://www.acm.org publishes the most widely cited code in computing. IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) — https://www.ieee.org and its Computer Society — https://www.computer.org publish one aimed at software engineering practice. CIPS (Canadian Information Processing Society) — https://www.cips.ca is the Canadian professional body. A code is not law and breaching one rarely carries legal penalty; its real value is that it gives a practitioner something external to point at. "I would prefer not to" is weak in a meeting. "This breaches a clause of the code I am bound by" is considerably stronger.

§1.4.8 — Privacy law you must be able to name. FIP (Fair Information Practices) is not a law but the principle set underlying nearly all of them: say what you collect and why, collect only what that purpose needs, let people see and correct their own data, secure it, be accountable for it.

  • Canada, federal — PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act), overseen by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada — https://www.priv.gc.ca.
  • Québec — the private-sector regime substantially amended by legislation commonly called Law 25, phased in from 2022 to 2024, adding a designated person responsible for privacy, privacy impact assessments, confidentiality-incident reporting and rights around automated decisions. Regulator: Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec — https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca.
  • European Union — GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), in force since May 2018, reaching organisations outside the EU that handle EU residents' data. Full text at EUR-Lex — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj; official explanation from the European Commission — https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection_en and the European Data Protection Supervisor — https://www.edps.europa.eu.
  • United States — no single federal statute; state law applies, of which CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act) is the most influential, administered by the California Privacy Protection Agency — https://cppa.ca.gov.
  • International — the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) — https://www.oecd.org privacy guidelines of 1980 are the direct ancestor of most of the above; NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) — https://www.nist.gov publishes a Privacy Framework and a Cybersecurity Framework, both free.

Two mechanisms to know by name: informed consent means agreement given with knowledge of the facts, so consent buried in twelve thousand words is agreement without knowledge whatever was clicked; and opt-in versus opt-out, where the default decides the outcome for most people, which makes the choice of default an ethical decision rather than an interface decision.

§1.4.9 — Intellectual property. Trade secret protects any confidential formula, method or compilation for as long as it stays secret — and one leak ends the protection permanently. Copyright protects the expression of an idea for decades beyond the author's life, and never the underlying idea. Patent protects a novel, useful, non-obvious invention for roughly twenty years from filing, at the price of public disclosure of exactly how it works. For software the distinction that matters: copyright protects how you wrote it, not what it does — someone may lawfully read your program, understand the idea and implement it in their own code. Bodies: WIPO (World Intellectual Property Organization) — https://www.wipo.int and the Canadian Intellectual Property Office — https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en.

§1.4.10 — System quality and quality of life. No system is error-free, and beyond a point the cost of removing the next defect exceeds any plausible benefit — which makes "how good is good enough?" an ethical decision, because the answer depends on what the system can do to people. Three sources of poor quality: software defects (reducible, never to zero), hardware or facility failures (reducible with redundancy, which costs money), and poor input data quality (by a wide margin the largest source of wrong answers in real organisations, and the least attended to because it is nobody's job). Quality-of-life effects: dissolved work/personal boundaries, dependence and vulnerability, computer crime, employment effects, equity and access (a service delivered only through an app excludes people who cannot use apps, and that exclusion is invisible to designers because they all have phones), and health.

§1.1 — What infrastructure is. IT (Information Technology) infrastructure is the shared set of physical and software resources that every application in an organisation depends on but that no single application owns. Both halves matter. The payroll system does not own the network; the website does not own the storage array. Because it is shared, when infrastructure fails it fails for everyone simultaneously — which is why infrastructure decisions are management decisions rather than technical ones, and why they carry long contracts and long depreciation schedules.

§1.2 — The seven components. Learn them as an assessment checklist: computer hardware platforms; operating system platforms; enterprise software applications; data management and storage; networking and telecommunications; internet platforms; and consulting and system integration services. Component seven surprises students — consulting is not hardware. It is infrastructure because knowledge is a shared resource that every application depends on and none owns, which is precisely the definition. An organisation whose only competent administrator is one overworked contractor has an infrastructure weakness even if every machine is new, and that is a real, defensible assessment finding.

§1.3 — Four eras. Mainframe (one expensive machine, total control, slow change) → personal computer (power on every desk, control shattered, consistency collapsed) → client/server (shared valuable work on servers, interface work on desktops — still the shape of most business computing) → cloud and mobile (processing back to the centre, but someone else's centre, rented rather than owned, with a phone as the client). The lesson is not the dates. It is the force: computing power oscillates between centre and edge, and every swing trades control against flexibility. Nobody has solved this; only the price of the trade changes. A hospital and a design studio need opposite answers.

§1.4 — Virtualization. Running software that pretends to be a computer, on top of a real computer. The pretend computer is a VM (Virtual Machine) with its own apparent CPU (Central Processing Unit), RAM (Random Access Memory), disk and OS (Operating System); the software that creates and manages them is a hypervisor. Three economic reasons it matters: utilisation (a physical server running one application typically uses 10–20 percent of capacity; the rest is bought, powered, cooled and wasted); speed (a physical server takes weeks, a VM takes seconds, because a VM is a file); and the industry-changing one — because a VM is a file it can be copied, moved, paused and destroyed, so a provider can rent you a machine for eleven minutes and delete it. Every "pay for what you use" model rests on that single property. A container shares the host's operating system instead of simulating its own — milliseconds instead of seconds, megabytes instead of gigabytes — and the trade is isolation versus efficiency.

Read the Data row. In every model including SaaS (Software as a Service), the data is yours. When a provider is breached, the organisation still notifies its customers, still faces the regulator, still absorbs the reputational damage. You can rent the machines; you cannot rent away responsibility for the data. That sentence links Lesson 1 to Lesson 4 and appears in examinations. IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) = maximum control, maximum work. PaaS (Platform as a Service) = less control, much less work. SaaS = almost no control, almost no work. The best model is the one whose split of responsibility matches the skills actually on staff — an organisation with no systems administrator that buys IaaS has bought a problem.

§1.6 — Emerging technologies, judged honestly. Edge computing removes round-trip delay and works when links are unreliable, at the cost of many small sites to secure and patch instead of one large one. Containers and orchestration make software behave the same on a laptop and in production and turn scaling into configuration, at the cost of a significant new layer of complexity requiring genuine expertise. Machine learning platforms make prediction from large data sets practical, at the cost of specialised hardware, large data requirements and outputs that are hard to explain to an auditor. Quantum computing changes what is computable for a narrow problem class, is not generally available or general-purpose, and is a future threat to some current encryption. The three assessment questions, in order: What problem that we actually have does this solve? What does it cost including the people? What happens if the vendor disappears or triples the price? That third one is lock-in, the cost nobody puts in the proposal.

§2.1 — The five diseases of the traditional file environment. Data redundancy (the same fact stored in several places); data inconsistency (those copies disagree and nobody knows which is right); program-data dependence (changing the data format breaks the programs); lack of flexibility (any new question needs new programming); poor security and sharing (no central place to control who sees what). Look hard at the fifth: when data is scattered, security is unenforceable in principle, not merely in practice, because there is no single place to stand and say "this person may see this and not that." Centralising does not automatically make data secure, but scattering makes security impossible. That asymmetry is permanent.

§2.2 — What a database is. A collection of data organised so it can be efficiently stored, retrieved and updated by many users and applications at once, under rules the storage system itself enforces. The last clause is the whole difference from a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet accepts "Montreal", "Montréal", "MTL" and "montreal " in one column because it has no opinion. A database can be told the value must come from a defined list and will refuse anything else — not warn, not highlight. A spreadsheet stores what you typed; a database stores only what is allowed. The enforcing software is a DBMS (Database Management System); when it organises data into tables that reference each other it is an RDBMS (Relational Database Management System).

§2.3 — The relational model. Four ideas. The table — named columns, unnamed rows, every row describing one thing of the same kind. The primary key — a column or combination whose value differs for every row and is never empty; two students can genuinely both be named Maria Silva, but they cannot both be student number 20250417. The foreign key — a column holding another table's primary key value; that is the relationship in "relational", and it is also a rule, because the database refuses a loan record pointing at member 9999 when no member 9999 exists. That rule is referential integrity and it is the single most valuable thing a database does for you. Normalisation — splitting data into tables so that every fact is stored in exactly one place. Stated bluntly: if you have to change something in two places to keep it true, the design is wrong. The formal normal forms are simply a reliable procedure for reaching that state.

§2.4 — The keys, formally. Primary key (uniquely identifies each row, never null). Foreign key (holds another table's primary key; you cannot reference what does not exist). Candidate key (any column that could have served as primary key). Composite key (a primary key made of two or more columns together). Surrogate key (an artificial, usually auto-incrementing value with no business meaning, which stays stable when business facts change). The classic exam trap: using an email address as a primary key. Ask what happens when someone changes their email — every referencing row in every other table must update simultaneously — and the case for surrogate keys makes itself.

§2.5 — SQL. SQL (Structured Query Language) is how you talk to a relational database. Five statements do almost everything: CREATE TABLE, INSERT, SELECT, UPDATE, DELETE. Two additions turn a lookup into business intelligence: JOIN combines rows from two tables on a matching key, answering questions that span tables; GROUP BY with an aggregate (COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX) collapses many rows into one summary row, answering "how many", "how much" and "on average". The line to remember: a SELECT with a JOIN and a GROUP BY is not a database operation, it is a management report. Dashboards and analytics suites are a nicer window onto exactly that query.

§2.6 — The BI pipeline. BI (Business Intelligence) turns stored data into decisions. Five stages, each with a characteristic failure: source systems (record what is convenient rather than what is needed); ETL — extract, transform, load (transformation rules undocumented, so nobody can reproduce the numbers); data warehouse (a year out of date and nobody trusts it); analysis and reporting (beautiful dashboard answering a question nobody asked); decision (the number contradicts the manager's intuition and is quietly ignored). Notice the shape: only the stale warehouse is really technical. The rest are failures of definition, which tells you where to look first when a BI project disappoints. A data warehouse stores cleaned structured historical data with structure applied before storing; a data lake stores raw data of any shape and applies structure when you read it, which is flexible and is also how organisations produce a "data swamp" nobody can search. The difference is simply when you pay the organising cost.

§2.7 — Data governance. The policies and responsibilities answering four questions: who owns each piece of data (not who stores it — who is accountable for it being right); what may it be used for (consent and purpose limitation are legal requirements, not preferences); how long is it kept (data you no longer need is pure liability — it cannot help you and it can be stolen); and what does "correct" mean for this field (someone must decide whether an address is billing or residence, and write it down). Governance is where an information-management assessment either finds nothing wrong or finds everything wrong, and it is invisible until you look. Reference points: Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada — https://www.priv.gc.ca, Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec — https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca, EUR-Lex, full GDPR text — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj, and ISO/IEC 27001, International Organization for Standardization — https://www.iso.org/standard/27001.

§3.1 — What a network is. Two or more devices connected so they can exchange data — true and almost useless. The workable definition: a network is an agreement. A set of shared rules called protocols that let devices built by different companies in different decades exchange data without knowing anything about each other. Wires have been solved for a century; the remarkable part is that a 2026 phone talks to software designed in the 1980s over infrastructure owned by dozens of companies who never signed a contract with each other. A protocol is a rule about the format and order of messages. Nothing more mysterious.

§3.2 — The four layers. The TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol) model: 4. Application (the meaning of the message — HTTP, HTTPS, DNS, SMTP); 3. Transport (getting the whole message there correctly — TCP, UDP, adding port numbers, sequence numbers, error checks); 2. Internet (finding a route across many networks — IP, ICMP, adding source and destination addresses); 1. Link (moving bits across one physical hop — Ethernet, Wi-Fi, adding hardware addresses and error detection). Layering is the actual invention: each layer talks only to the layers directly above and below, so an entire layer can be replaced without touching any other. Wi-Fi was added to the world's networks without rewriting a single web browser. As data descends the layers each one wraps it in a header — encapsulation — and the receiver unwraps in reverse.

§3.3 — Following one request, end to end. Eight steps, and you should be able to reproduce them from memory because they are a diagnostic checklist for the rest of your career: (1) name lookup via DNS (Domain Name System); (2) route selection comparing destination against your own address and subnet mask, sending off-network traffic to the default gateway; (3) link-layer delivery to the first hop; (4) routing across the internet, where each router reads the destination, consults its routing table and forwards one hop closer — no single router knows the whole path; (5) transport-layer connection, where TCP establishes a connection with a three-message handshake and numbers every chunk; (6) security negotiation, where TLS (Transport Layer Security) negotiates keys for HTTPS; (7) application request and response over HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol); (8) unwrap and render. Each step is a distinct failure mode with a distinct symptom: name works but address does not → DNS; local works but nothing outside → gateway or subnet mask; no connection at all → cable, radio or access point; some sites work → upstream provider or firewall; connects then stalls → congestion or packet loss; certificate warning → certificate expiry or clock mismatch; error page with a number → the server or application itself.

§3.4 — Wireless. Wi-Fi (Wireless Fidelity) is defined by the IEEE 802.11 family published by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers — https://www.ieee.org, with certification by the Wi-Fi Alliance — https://www.wi-fi.org. Three properties make it behave unlike a cable. It is a shared medium — switched Ethernet gives each device its own path; Wi-Fi devices on a channel take turns in the same air and lose time to the taking-turns process itself, so the number on the box is a ceiling, not a promise. Interference — radio does not stop at your walls and neither does anyone else's. Channels overlap — in the 2.4 GHz band, North American channels 1 to 11 are wider than their spacing, so only channels 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap each other. That single fact resolves an enormous share of real Wi-Fi complaints. Realistic throughput, not the advertised figure, is the column to assess against: roughly 40–100 Mbps for 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4), 200–600 Mbps for 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5), 400–1200 Mbps for 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6/6E), and 1–4 Gbps for 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7).

§3.5 — The vocabulary of capacity. Bandwidth — maximum data per second the link can carry. Throughput — what you actually achieve, always lower. Latency — time for one packet to arrive, measured in milliseconds, governing how responsive things feel. Jitter — variation in latency between packets, governing whether voice and video sound smooth. The critical insight: bandwidth and latency are independent. A satellite link has enormous bandwidth and terrible latency; a short copper cable has modest bandwidth and superb latency. Buying more bandwidth does not fix a latency problem, and that misunderstanding is one of the most expensive in the industry. For a video call, latency and jitter dominate. For a large file transfer, bandwidth dominates. Assess the application before you assess the link.

§4.1 — What security protects. Security is not a product. Firewalls and antivirus subscriptions are controls — tools you might choose. Security itself is a management discipline for deciding how much loss you are willing to accept, and then spending accordingly. It protects three properties, the CIA triad: Confidentiality (only authorised people can read it), Integrity (the data is correct and unaltered without authorisation), Availability (the system is there when needed). Two consequences students miss. First, the three trade against each other — the most confidential system is one nobody can access, the most available is one with no login — so there is no configuration maximising all three, and every real decision is a chosen position on that trade-off. Second, availability is a security property: a system that is down is a security failure as surely as one that is breached, which is why a backup is a security control and why network redundancy is a security topic.

§4.2 — The vocabulary of risk. Asset (something of value you are protecting). Threat (something that could cause harm). Vulnerability (a weakness the threat could use). Risk (the combination of likelihood and impact). Control (something you do to reduce it). The relationship as one sentence: a threat exploits a vulnerability to damage an asset, and a control reduces either the likelihood or the impact. Notice the implication — you cannot remove threats, since criminals exist regardless; you can remove vulnerabilities and add controls. Everything within your power sits on the right-hand side of that sentence.

§4.3 — Threats you will actually meet. Phishing — a message impersonating a trusted party persuades a person to surrender credentials or run something; the controls are MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication) plus a reporting culture that never blames the clicker. Ransomware — malicious software encrypts files and demands payment; the control is tested, offline backups, not detection. Credential stuffing — passwords stolen from one service tried on another; MFA and unique passwords. Insider action — an authorised person misuses access, deliberately or not; least privilege, logging, separation of duties. Unpatched software — a publicly known flaw exploited because the fix was never applied; a patch process with a deadline. Misconfiguration — a storage bucket or database port left open; configuration review, default-deny, automated checks. DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) — overwhelming traffic makes a service unavailable; upstream filtering and capacity, arranged before the attack. Look at the control column: almost none are exotic. The controls that prevent most real incidents are unglamorous, well understood and frequently not implemented, because they need sustained ordinary effort rather than a purchase. On the phishing note specifically: organisations that punish clickers get fewer reports, not fewer clicks, which increases attacker dwell time. Blame reduces reporting; reduced reporting increases damage.

§4.4 — Defence in depth. Layered controls arranged so any single failure is survivable. Seven layers from outside in: policy and people (training, a clear reporting path, joiner/leaver process); physical (locked rooms, visitor control); perimeter (firewall, segmentation); network (segmentation between departments, monitoring); endpoint (patching, disk encryption, malware protection); application and identity (MFA, least privilege, input validation); data (encryption at rest, tested backups, retention limits). Layer seven deserves emphasis: a tested backup is the only control that works after every other control has failed. Note tested. An untested backup is not a control, it is a hope — and backups fail silently for months, discovered at the worst possible moment. "We have backups" is not an answer to an auditor; "we restored 40 GB in 90 minutes on the third of last month, and here is the log" is.

§4.5 — The arithmetic that makes security a management decision. SLE (Single Loss Expectancy) = what one occurrence costs. ARO (Annualized Rate of Occurrence) = how many times per year you expect it. ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy) = SLE × ARO — the amount you expect to lose to this specific risk in an average year. Risk reduction = ALE before the control − ALE after. ROSI (Return on Security Investment) = (risk reduction − annual cost of control) ÷ annual cost of control. If the risk reduction is smaller than the control's cost, you should not buy the control, and being able to say so with arithmetic behind it is what makes you credible when you say the opposite about something else. Two honest caveats: the inputs are estimates — ARO especially is judgement, and the formula does not manufacture certainty, it makes assumptions visible and arguable; and it handles frequent moderate losses well and rare catastrophic ones badly — an event with a one-in-two-hundred chance of ending the organisation has a modest ALE and an unacceptable risk, and for those you buy insurance or avoid the activity. Knowing when the formula stops applying separates an analyst from a calculator.

§4.6 — When it goes wrong. Six phases, referenced to NIST SP 800-61 — https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/final: preparation (contacts, logging, backups, a written plan — the only phase whose timing you control, and it determines how the other five go); identification (recognising this is an incident, not a glitch); containment (short-term then long-term); eradication (removing the cause, not the symptom); recovery (restoring service and confirming it is genuinely clean); lessons learned (a written, blameless review that changes something — a meeting producing no change to a system, process or budget did not happen, it was a conversation). Two frameworks by name: the NIST Cybersecurity Framework — https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework, organising security into Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover; and ISO/IEC 27001 — https://www.iso.org/standard/27001, the certifiable international standard for an information security management system. National guidance: Canadian Centre for Cyber Security — https://www.cyber.gc.ca, Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency — https://www.cisa.gov, European Union Agency for Cybersecurity — https://www.enisa.europa.eu. For web application security specifically, the OWASP Foundation — https://owasp.org.

§1.1 — The two goals. Operational excellence means doing the everyday work — taking orders, moving products, sending invoices — faster, cheaper and with fewer errors than before and than competitors. It is efficiency you can measure. Customer intimacy means knowing customers individually and using that knowledge to serve them personally rather than treating them as strangers on every visit. Both are information problems, which is why organisations buy large systems to reach them.

§1.2 — Information silos. Every department bought its own software over the years; none of them talk. Each holds a private copy of data. The guaranteed consequence is that the copies drift apart: the warehouse count says 40 units, sales already sold 2, accounting recorded the sale at last year's price. Ask three departments how the business is doing and you get three answers with no way to prove which is true — plus hours of re-typing, each keystroke a fresh chance to introduce error.

§1.3 — ERP. An ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) system replaces separate departmental programs with one integrated system built around one shared central database. Hold on to that phrase, because it is the entire idea. When the counter records a sale, the inventory count drops, the revenue is recorded at the current official price, and the customer's record updates — automatically, instantly, without re-typing. One real-world event, one entry, every department updated. When every number comes from the same place, every report agrees with itself; that agreement is often called a single source of truth, and it is what makes operational excellence possible. ERP is sold as modules — finance, inventory, human resources, manufacturing, sales — activated as needed. Known vendors include SAP — https://www.sap.com, Oracle — https://www.oracle.com and Microsoft — https://www.microsoft.com with Dynamics 365; a well-known open-source option is Odoo — https://www.odoo.com.

§1.4 — SCM. An ERP organises information inside one company; SCM (Supply Chain Management) shares it across the chain of suppliers, manufacturers, warehouses, transporters, stores and customers, so supply matches real demand. Two facts. The bullwhip effect: when companies in a chain do not share real sales data, each guesses and each adds a safety margin, and those margins stack level after level, so a small change in actual customer buying becomes a much larger swing in orders further up the chain — and the proven remedy is exactly what SCM provides, sharing true demand numbers with every level. Push versus pull: in a push model companies produce against forecasts and push product toward stores; in a pull model actual purchases trigger production and replenishment. Modern SCM moves firms from push toward pull, because real numbers beat guesses.

§1.5 — CRM. CRM (Customer Relationship Management) gathers every interaction with every customer — purchases, emails, support calls, complaints, loyalty points — into a single customer view, so sales sees what a customer already owns, marketing can match offers to actual history, and service sees the whole story on the first call. Two numbers managers care about: churn rate, the percentage of customers who stop buying in a period (lower is better), and CLTV (Customer Lifetime Value), an estimate of total profit from one customer over the whole relationship, which tells you how much it is worth spending to keep them. Vendors: Salesforce — https://www.salesforce.com, HubSpot — https://www.hubspot.com, Zoho — https://www.zoho.com.

§1.6 — The honest paragraph. Enterprise applications are expensive — licences, consultants, training, months of work. They usually force the company to change its work habits to match the software's built-in processes, and people resist that. Once the whole company runs on one vendor's system, leaving becomes very hard and very costly — that trap is switching costs. Most ERP projects that run late do so for human reasons, not technical ones. Installing the software is the easy half; changing how people work is the hard half.

§2.1 — What e-commerce is. Buying and selling goods or services over the internet, including the marketing and payment around the transaction. The lazy definition — "a store, but online" — is wrong because a digital market has properties no physical store can have: ubiquity (available everywhere, all the time); global reach (a one-person shop in Montréal sells to Osaka with the same click); universal standards (every site speaks the same internet standards, so the cost of reaching the market is tiny compared with renting a storefront); information richness and density (video, reviews, comparisons and full specifications, cheaply, for everyone at once — which reduces information asymmetry, the gap between what the seller knows and what the buyer knows); personalization (the store greets you and recommends from your history, for millions simultaneously); and interactivity and social technology (customers talk back, and their content becomes part of the product page).

§2.2 — What changes in a digital market. Search costs collapse — comparing sellers is nearly free, so sellers compete harder on price and quality. Transaction and menu costs drop — a transaction cost is the cost of making the deal happen, a menu cost is the cost of changing your prices (named for reprinting restaurant menus); online, changing a price is editing a database field, which is why dynamic pricing exists and why flight prices move between two visits. Middlemen can be skipped — removing intermediate layers is disintermediation, though new intermediaries appear: platforms, payment processors and delivery apps each take a percentage.

§2.3 — Digital goods. A product delivered as pure data — an e-book, a stream, a software licence, a PDF (Portable Document Format) study guide. Marginal cost is the cost of producing and delivering one more unit. For a printed book it is real money on every copy forever; for the same book as a file it is a few cents of server and bandwidth, essentially zero, whether it is copy ten or copy ten million. The first copy can still be very expensive to make. This single fact explains why streaming subscriptions can be cheap, why software firms scale so fast, why piracy is such a fight, and why pricing digital goods is a strategy question rather than a cost calculation.

§2.4 — Who sells to whom, and how the money is made. B2C (Business-to-Consumer), B2B (Business-to-Business), C2C (Consumer-to-Consumer), plus m-commerce (mobile commerce) — any of the above done on a phone — and social commerce, buying inside a social network. Revenue models: sales, subscription, advertising, freemium, transaction fee, affiliate. A platform such as Shopify — https://www.shopify.com, a Canadian company, exists precisely so one person can open a B2C store with a chosen revenue model in an afternoon. Canadian online stores collecting personal information must respect PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act), explained by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada — https://www.priv.gc.ca.

§3.1 — DIKW. Data are raw recorded facts with no context. Information is data organised so it means something to a person. Knowledge is information combined with experience and judgment so it can guide action — and it is the expensive one, because it took a person time, mistakes and attention to acquire. Some models add wisdom, knowing which knowledge to apply when, giving the sequence its common name, the DIKW (Data–Information–Knowledge–Wisdom) hierarchy. For this course, keep the first three strictly separate.

§3.2 — Tacit versus explicit. Explicit knowledge has been written down in transferable form — manuals, procedures, FAQ (Frequently Asked Questions) pages, checklists, well-commented code. It can be stored, searched, copied and taught, and it survives its author's departure. Tacit knowledge lives in a person's head and hands: the technician who hears a fan and knows which bearing is failing; the agent who senses which customer needs patience and which needs speed. It was learned by experience, is hard to write down completely, and — the critical property — it walks out of the building whenever its owner does, whether by resignation, retirement, sick leave or a holiday timed with your worst outage. The question that funds every knowledge management project: when your most experienced person leaves, what exactly does your organisation still know?

§3.3 — The KM value chain. Four steps that repeat as a cycle, not a line. Acquire — capture what the expert explains, the solved case, the decision and its reasons; this is where tacit becomes explicit and it is the step organisations skip when they are "too busy", which is always. Store — one organised, backed-up, findable place, not seventeen personal folders and a departed employee's inbox. Share — search, categories, links from the ticket system, onboarding reading lists; stored-but-unfindable knowledge is stored-but-dead. Apply — use it on the next real case and measure that use; applying reveals what is missing, which starts the next round of acquiring. Supporting system families: ECM (Enterprise Content Management) for documents and records; LMS (Learning Management Systems); wikis and internal knowledge bases; and KWS (Knowledge Work Systems) — specialist workstations such as CAD (Computer-Aided Design) tools or VR (Virtual Reality) training environments.

§3.4 — Intelligent techniques, and the course rule on AI. Classic expert systems stored a specialist's rules as if–then statements. Machine learning finds patterns in large data sets without being given the rules. LLMs (Large Language Models) — the technology behind AI (Artificial Intelligence) chat assistants — summarise documents, draft answers from a knowledge base and act as a natural-language search box over everything a company has written down. They are genuinely useful and genuinely risky, because they answer fluently even when they are wrong, and fluency is not accuracy. The course rule, which applies to your labs, your future job and your use of AI on any assessment:

The model drafts. The analyst decides. The analyst's name goes on the record.

An AI system may help produce a draft, but you verify it, you take responsibility, and your name signs it. Handing in unverified machine output fails the ethics test and, sooner or later, the accuracy test.

§4.1 — Three types of decision. Structured decisions are repetitive and follow a clear, complete rule — an unopened item returned within thirty days with a receipt is refunded — and can be fully automated. Unstructured decisions are novel and important with no formula; data helps, but judgment, values and risk tolerance carry the decision, and they belong to senior management. Semi-structured decisions sit between: part of the problem is calculable and part needs judgment. Most middle-management decisions live here, and this is exactly where computer support pays off most.

§4.2 — Simon's four stages. Herbert Simon, winner of the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, 1978 — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1978/simon/facts/, described decision making as intelligence (discover and understand the problem — "intelligence" as in intelligence service, gathering facts, not IQ); design (invent and model possible solutions); choice (pick one, using the models and the numbers); implementation (carry it out and check whether it worked, feeding the result back into stage one). Every system in this lesson supports one or more of those four stages.

§4.3 — Matching system to decision type. TPS (Transaction Processing Systems) record raw daily events and are the source of all data below them; your ERP is built on them. MIS (Management Information Systems) produce routine scheduled reports, serve structured decisions, and answer "what happened?" BI (Business Intelligence) is the umbrella term for analysing business data — dashboards, drill-downs, trends — with tools such as Microsoft Power BI — https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/power-platform/products/power-bi and Tableau — https://www.tableau.com; a dashboard shows the vital numbers, the KPIs (Key Performance Indicators), on one screen, and drill-down means clicking a total to see the detail underneath. DSS (Decision-Support Systems) serve semi-structured decisions, and their signature move is what-if analysis (also called sensitivity analysis): change an assumption and the model instantly recomputes, so the manager plays with assumptions before risking real money. ESS (Executive Support Systems) serve senior management and unstructured decisions with one screen of critical internal and external KPIs plus drill-down, often organised by the balanced scorecard method, which deliberately tracks finance, customers, internal processes and learning/growth so executives do not steer by a single number.

§4.4 — Two warnings. Data quality: a beautiful dashboard fed by wrong data produces confident wrong decisions. The engineering phrase is GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out) — output quality can never exceed input quality — which is why the "one shared database, numbers that agree" of Lesson 1 is not cosmetic, it is the precondition for everything here. Automated decisions inherit our mistakes: when structured decisions are fully automated — loan approvals, résumé screening, fraud flags — the rules and the historical data they learned from can contain bias, and the system repeats it tirelessly and at scale. Treat automated decisions as something to monitor and audit, never to set and forget. Whoever deploys the system owns its mistakes.

§3.1 — What "building" includes. An information system is not only software. It includes people, procedures, data, controls, technology and the way those elements are organised to support work and decisions — so building one is broader than writing code. A programmer can build a booking form in an afternoon; the real questions are who may reserve, how availability is calculated, what happens when two requests arrive simultaneously, what data must be retained, who can cancel, how staff override rules, how users are notified and what happens when the system is unavailable. A correct process starts from a business need, not a programming language, and moves through analysis, design, implementation, testing, conversion and continuing operation — with a deliberate return from operation to analysis, because real systems keep changing after launch.

§3.2 — Four levels of organisational change. Automation — the system performs an existing task, and the process is otherwise unchanged. Process rationalisation — unnecessary steps, duplicate approvals and repeated rework are simplified. Business-process redesign — the order, ownership or structure of work changes, so staff time is reserved for exceptions and the control logic of the process is different. Change to the operating model — the largest form, raising questions about ownership, budgets, shared data, policy and support across units. The assessment question is not "how advanced is the technology?" It is "how much organisational change is required, who is affected, and has that change been planned?" Large organisational change creates higher adoption, training, governance and implementation risk even when the software is straightforward.

§3.3 — Systems analysis. A requirement describes a capability, rule, constraint, quality expectation or control the system must satisfy. Functional requirements state what the system must do — including "the system must prevent two active reservations for the same item and period", which is functional because it defines behaviour. Non-functional requirements state a quality or constraint: performance, availability, access control. Requirements must be testable — "the system must be user friendly" is untestable, while "a first-time student can search, select an available item and submit a reservation without staff assistance after reading the one-page instruction" can be put in front of users. Requirements need priorities (Must, Should, Could, not in this release), and the purpose is not to make features disappear but to force an explicit decision about the first usable release. Analysis also defines data requirements and business rules — what counts as an item, a reservation, an available slot, an overdue return — because ambiguous definitions become divergent implementations. And it identifies stakeholders: the payer is not always the user, the people entering data are not always the people relying on reports, and a privacy officer who never uses the system may hold mandatory requirements.

§3.4 — Feasibility. Technical — does the organisation have, or can it acquire and support, the technology and skills? Ongoing support, upgrades, security and recovery are part of the question, not just installation. Economic — do expected benefits justify the complete cost, which includes development or licence, implementation, integration, training, data conversion, support, infrastructure, vendor fees and staff time? Operational — will it work in the real organisation, with real roles, real policies and realistic behaviour? A technically strong system fails when the design assumes busy staff will complete optional fields that give them no immediate value. Schedule — can it be delivered in the required time, and if not, does management reduce scope, use a package, phase the rollout or move the date? Do not collapse these into a single score without explanation: a strong overall score must never hide an unresolved mandatory legal requirement.

§3.5 — Systems design. Analysis asks what; design asks how. Design covers interface behaviour, data structures, processing rules, integrations, access control, logging, error handling, backup and recovery, and operating procedures. Two classic mistakes. Designing before the requirement is clear — deciding "we need a mobile application" is a design choice; the requirement may simply be "students must be able to reserve from their own devices", which a responsive website could satisfy more cheaply. Confusing a screen with a process — a Reserve button does not define validation rules, data changes, confirmation behaviour, conflict handling, the audit record, the cancellation rule or the error response. Security and privacy belong in design, not bolted on afterwards; public guidance is published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology — https://www.nist.gov.

§3.6 — Choosing an approach. Traditional SDLC (Systems Development Life Cycle) — defined stages and formal approvals; strong when requirements are stable, documentation matters, interfaces are fixed or contracts require stage outputs; weak because a change discovered late is expensive. Prototyping — build an early version so users react to something concrete; useful when the objective is understood but the workflow is not; the danger is shortcuts that are fine for learning and unsafe in production, so management must decide whether the prototype is disposable, evolutionary or being hardened. Agile iterative development — small increments, frequent feedback, reprioritised work; reduces the risk of building unwanted functionality; not an excuse for no documentation, architecture or control, and it requires active decision makers, a prioritised backlog, clear acceptance criteria and a disciplined definition of done. Low-code or end-user development — valuable for small local problems; the assessment questions are who owns the application when the creator leaves, how access is controlled, how data quality is maintained, how changes are tested, and whether dozens of local applications are creating uncontrolled duplication. Application packages and SaaS (Software as a Service) — appropriate when a standard business process will do; the issue becomes fit, and heavy customisation makes upgrades difficult, so sometimes the correct management decision is to change the internal process to match the package. Outsourcing — provides skills and capacity, and does not transfer accountability for business outcomes, data governance, contract management or the ability to operate the system afterwards; knowledge transfer and exit arrangements are requirements, not optional contract language.

§3.7 — Testing, conversion, production, maintenance. Unit testing checks small components; integration testing checks components and external services together; system testing checks the complete system against requirements; UAT (User Acceptance Testing) checks whether intended users can complete the agreed business work and whether the result is acceptable. Testing must trace back to requirements — if requirement R3 forbids two reservations of the same item for the same period, at least one test must attempt exactly that conflict, which is what a requirements traceability table is for. Conversion moves from old to new: direct changeover (fast, higher operational risk, because the old system stops), parallel operation (lower risk, higher workload, two systems running), pilot (first use limited to one group or location), phased (functions or groups move in stages). After launch comes production and maintenance; maintenance is not evidence of failure, since every useful system needs continuing management. The assessment questions are whether maintenance is controlled, documented, funded, tested and prioritised.

§4.1 — Why projects. A project is temporary work undertaken to produce a defined result. System work is managed as a project because it has an objective, a start, an intended finish, a budget or capacity limit, assigned responsibilities and uncertainty to control. The professional body is the Project Management Institute — https://www.pmi.org. For information systems specifically, technical work and organisational work depend on each other: a team can finish a feature while the organisation still needs data cleaning, policy approval, training, vendor coordination, security review or operational readiness. A project plan containing only programming tasks is incomplete. A useful project definition answers six questions: what result will be delivered; what is included and excluded; who owns decisions; when major results are required; what resources are available; and what evidence will demonstrate completion.

§4.2 — Scope and the WBS. Scope states the intended outcome, main deliverables, boundaries, assumptions and exclusions — it is not a long feature list. "Build a registration portal" identifies a product but not scope: does it include payment, badges, identity integration, speaker management, public visitors, production hosting, content creation, post-event training and support? Without explicit boundaries, stakeholders hold different assumptions while believing they agree, and the conflict that surfaces later actually started when scope was left implicit. A WBS (Work Breakdown Structure) decomposes the result into smaller deliverables and work packages so the complete work is visible enough to estimate, assign, sequence and control. For a small portal it might contain requirements confirmation, interface design, data design, build, integration, test preparation, UAT, deployment, training and closeout — and note how many of those are not programming.

§4.3 — Schedule logic. A dependency exists when one task relies on another. A milestone is a significant event or decision point with zero duration, describing an observable event ("requirements baseline approved", "production go-live approved"), not an activity. The critical path is the longest dependency path through the network; it determines the earliest possible finish, and a task on it has no scheduling flexibility in the current plan. Two cautions. Non-critical tasks are not unimportant — one can consume its flexibility and become critical, and a task can be critical for quality, compliance or safety while sitting off the schedule's critical path. And the total duration is not the sum of all task durations, because parallel work exists; the dependency network determines the result. Worked example from the lecture: A (confirm requirements, 2 days) → B (screen design, 3 days) and C (database build, 4 days) in parallel → D (integration, 3 days, needs both) → E (UAT, 2 days). A-C-D-E is eleven working days and sets the earliest finish; A-B-D-E is ten. One day of delay on B changes nothing; one day on C moves the finish to twelve.

§4.4 — Cost and progress. A project can report spending half its budget without telling you whether half the work is done. PV (Planned Value) is the budgeted value of work that should have been completed by the status date. EV (Earned Value) is the budgeted value of work actually completed. AC (Actual Cost) is what has actually been spent for the work performed. Schedule variance = EV − PV; cost variance = EV − AC; SPI (Schedule Performance Index) = EV ÷ PV; CPI (Cost Performance Index) = EV ÷ AC. Worked figures from the lecture: PV CAD 10,000, EV CAD 7,600, AC CAD 8,600 gives schedule variance −2,400, cost variance −1,000, SPI 0.76 and CPI approximately 0.88 — both below 1.00. These indicators are prompts for investigation, not explanations: a low SPI could come from late requirements approval, technical problems, unavailable staff, unrealistic estimates or scope change, and the number tells management where to ask questions, not what the cause is.

§4.5 — Risk. A risk is an uncertain event or condition that could affect objectives; an issue is a problem that has already happened. Risks need preparation, issues need resolution. A useful risk statement names cause, event and effect — "because the team has not used the selected database integration before, the prototype may fail the required performance test, which could delay integration and UAT" gives management something to act on, where "database delay" does not. A risk register records risk, probability, impact, owner, response, trigger and status; the score is a prioritisation aid only, a low-probability catastrophic risk may still need a response, and a high score with no named owner is not controlled. Responses: avoidance (change the plan so the risk no longer exists), reduction (lower probability or impact), transfer (assign financial or delivery consequences by insurance or contract — though accountability is not fully transferred), acceptance (knowingly tolerate, ideally with contingency planning).

§4.6 — Roles and authority. Projects often fail slowly because nobody knows who can decide. A sponsor owns the business case and major decisions; a project manager coordinates delivery and control; a product owner or business lead prioritises requirements in iterative delivery; technical leads decide within agreed authority; business users provide operational knowledge and acceptance evidence; security, privacy, finance, procurement and legal may hold approval or consultation roles. A RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) table clarifies who performs the work, who owns the final decision, who provides input before action and who is kept informed. A task should not have two people each believing they are the final accountable decision maker. The practical reason for the table is speed: when a defect appears two days before go-live, the team must already know who blocks the release, who investigates, who advises on risk and who must be told.

§4.7 — Change control. A requested change may be individually sensible and still require analysis before approval. The project manager should neither reject it because it was not in the plan nor accept it because it sounds useful; the correct action is impact analysis — what requirements change, what design changes, what code changes, what test cases are added, what security or privacy effects arise, what dependencies move, what it costs, and whether something else should be removed to protect the deadline. Change control is not designed to prevent change. It is designed to prevent invisible consequences. In iterative projects it can be lighter for uncommitted backlog items and stricter for changes touching an active iteration, a contractual baseline, architecture, security or the release date. "Agile" does not mean scope, cost and risk stop requiring decisions.

§4.8 — Quality and success. Traditional measures are scope, schedule and cost. Information-system success also needs quality, adoption, operational performance, security, data quality and business benefit. A project can finish on schedule and still fail the business objective; it can also exceed its original estimate for a justified, controlled and approved reason and still produce strong value. Post-deployment KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) might include completed registrations, registration error rate, failed transactions, support requests per hundred users, average resolution time and user task-completion rate — and the test of a KPI is simple: if nobody knows what action the metric would cause, it may not be useful.

§5.1 — What makes a system global. A system becomes a global management problem when it must support coordinated work across national, regional, legal, cultural, operational or time-zone boundaries. It is not defined by where the server sits: one application in one country is a global system if users, customers, suppliers, regulators, support teams or business units in several countries depend on it — and an organisation can run servers in several countries without having an integrated global system if each location works independently. The central tension is standardization (common processes, data definitions, security controls and software reduce duplication and ease coordination) against local responsiveness (laws, languages, payment methods, expectations, support hours and operating conditions vary). The correct decision is rarely "standardize everything" or "let every location choose"; the task is to identify what creates shared value when standardized and what genuinely requires variation.

§5.2 — Strategy and system configuration. A domestic exporter keeps core operations and management control at home while selling into foreign markets; systems are often highly centralized, which simplifies control and can produce weak local fit. A multinational gives substantial autonomy to country operations; systems become decentralized, buying local fit at the cost of duplication, inconsistent data and difficult integration. A franchiser standardizes a core product, brand or process while allowing controlled local differences; systems duplicate a common core with permitted extensions, and the management problem is stopping those extensions becoming incompatible. A transnational needs both global coordination and significant local responsiveness, typically requiring networked systems where shared global data and services coexist with regional capabilities — strong coordination, demanding governance. These are analytical tools, not labels every firm fits; the assessment question is whether the configuration matches how decisions and operations are actually managed.

§5.3 — Four configurations. Centralized — development, operation, data control or processing concentrated centrally; simplifies standards, security and master data; creates dependency and may not satisfy local legal or performance requirements. Duplicated — a common design or core platform copied to several locations; supports consistent processes with regional independence; the management problem is version control, because if every copy is modified differently it stops being a common system. Decentralized — locations choose or build their own; maximum autonomy, and commonly duplicate functionality, inconsistent definitions, incompatible interfaces, higher total support cost and weak global reporting. Networked — shared global services and data connected with regional or local services; for many modern organisations this is the practical target, and it requires the strongest governance because interfaces, responsibilities, data ownership and versioning must be coordinated across teams. Networked is not automatically best.

§5.4 — Global core versus controlled local variation. Write two explicit lists. The global core normally holds elements whose consistency creates value: identity standards, master data definitions, common security controls, core transaction rules, shared reporting definitions, common integration interfaces, global product or service identifiers. Controlled local variation holds elements that must differ for a justified reason: language, currency display, local tax logic, required invoice fields, permitted payment methods, regional support schedules, local regulatory notices, accessibility requirements and integrations with local public or financial systems. The word controlled carries the weight — local variation needs an owner, a documented reason, test evidence, a support model and an upgrade path, or the organisation quietly recreates a decentralized environment while everyone believes they run one global platform. Two review questions: what would break if this were standardized? and what would break if every region changed this independently?

§5.5 — Data protection and data location. Jurisdictions impose different obligations on collection, consent, purpose, access, retention, breach response, international transfers, processors and data-subject rights. Canadian federal guidance comes from the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada — https://www.priv.gc.ca; in Québec the authority is the Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec — https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca; the full GDPR text is published through EUR-Lex — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj. A global data-flow diagram must answer where data is collected, stored and backed up, which services process it, which countries support personnel access it from, and where reports or extracts are sent — because a system that appears to have one database also has data in logs, analytics services, support tools, email notifications, backups and third-party integrations. "Keep all data in one country" is not a complete privacy strategy: location is one factor alongside purpose, access, retention, security, lawful processing, contracts, user rights and transfer rules. Do not reduce privacy to server geography.

§5.6 — Language, culture, formats, accessibility. Localization is more than translating labels. Text length changes; address formats vary; names use different character sets and structures; date formats vary; decimal and thousands separators vary; reading direction can vary; currency symbols are ambiguous; legal notices may require specific wording; search and sorting rules differ by language. A system storing "first name" and "last name" may not fit every naming convention; a form assuming one postal-code format will reject valid addresses; 03/04/2026 means different dates in different conventions. These are design and data-quality issues, not cosmetics. Accessibility should be a core quality requirement rather than an optional regional feature; the World Wide Web Consortium — https://www.w3.org publishes the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. The examinable question is whether localization is a controlled requirement with testing, ownership and content maintenance — because a one-time translation goes stale the moment the source language changes.

§5.7 — Currency, payments, tax, financial integration. Distinguish display from accounting and settlement. A user may see a local-currency price while the accounting system records a different functional currency; exchange rates move; refund rules differ; payment methods vary by region; taxes are calculated differently; invoices require different fields; and reconciliation must connect the payment provider's result to the financial records. A technically successful checkout is not enough — the system needs end-to-end financial testing across quote, tax, authorization, settlement, accounting entry, refund, exception and reporting, with finance staff participating in requirements and acceptance rather than inspecting afterwards. Where an ERP is involved, integrations need data ownership and error-handling rules, because "the API (Application Programming Interface) call succeeded" does not prove the accounting result is correct.

§5.8 — Performance and regional architecture. A system performing well near its hosting region may respond slowly from another continent, and the only correct way to assess this is to measure from the regions where users actually work. "The system should be fast globally" is not testable; "the course-search result displays within two seconds for at least 95 percent of normal requests measured from approved test locations" is. Regional services, caching, content delivery, replicated data and local integrations improve performance and increase architecture and governance complexity: replication raises consistency and privacy questions, caching creates stale information, and regional failover needs testing rather than assumption. Availability also depends on support and recovery — a maintenance window that is overnight for headquarters may be peak business time elsewhere.

§5.9 — Time zones and support. Time zones affect monitoring, release coordination, incident response, batch processing, report cutoffs, deadlines, support coverage and the meaning of timestamps. Store precise timestamps in a consistent reference such as UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) and convert for display; local legal or operational records may still need local time and zone information. Daylight-saving changes make fixed offsets unreliable, so production systems should use recognised time-zone databases rather than permanently entered offsets. Support can be centralized, regional or follow-the-sun, where responsibility passes between teams as their working days begin and end — long coverage without one team working continuously, at the price of disciplined handoff records, shared ticket data, escalation rules and consistent procedures. A global system with no practical support during a region's working day is not operationally ready even if every technical test passed.

§5.10 — Governance, master data, integration. Shared technology creates shared consequences. Master data is the relatively stable identifiers and definitions many processes depend on — customer identifiers, product codes, location codes, account structures, course identifiers — and if each region defines the same concept differently, global reporting and integration become unreliable. Governance must identify data owners, process owners, architecture authority, security standards, local exception authority, release responsibilities and change approval, so that operational questions have answers: who can add a global customer attribute, who approves a regional integration, who decides whether a local exception becomes the global standard, who owns a defect appearing in only one region. Integration needs documented interfaces and clear ownership; APIs (Application Programming Interfaces), files and message queues can all work, and the management questions are constant — what data crosses, who owns its definition, how errors are detected, how retries work, how access is controlled, how changes are versioned, who responds when one side changes. A global system can fail through governance while every component is technically sound: a region changes a customer-status code for a local need, the change is not coordinated, and global analytics misclassifies customers. That is not a programming defect. It is an uncontrolled definition change.

§5.11 — Rollout. Base rollout on readiness evidence rather than the calendar. A pilot tests technical performance, procedures, support, training and data conversion before broader rollout; the best pilot is not always the easiest region, because an easy pilot proves very little, while a high-risk pilot may be too dangerous — choose one that provides useful evidence at acceptable risk. Wave deployment groups regions into stages by language, regulatory similarity, business unit, technical readiness or operational capacity, with entry criteria defined before a region joins a wave: completed privacy review, approved localization, successful integration tests, support staffing, completed data migration, signed business readiness. Readiness scores help compare regions but must not hide mandatory criteria — a region scoring 4.5 out of 5 does not launch if the missing item is a required regulatory approval. After rollout, measure outcomes by region as well as globally, because a global average hides local failure rates, support volume, response times, adoption gaps, reconciliation problems and data-quality issues.

Draws onWeek 01, §1.1.1, §1.1.2, §1.1.3, §1.1.6.
The scenario

A twelve-member bicycle co-operative in Montréal repairs and resells donated bicycles. Intake is recorded on a clipboard: date, donor initials, frame number, condition score out of five. Every Sunday the volunteer coordinator types the week's sheets into a spreadsheet, prints a two-page summary, and pins it to the workshop wall. Nobody has ever changed anything because of the summary. In eighteen months the co-operative has accumulated 3,100 rows. The board is proposing to buy a "proper system" because "our data is a mess."

The questions
  1. Does the co-operative currently operate an information system? Justify against the definition.
  2. Which of the four system types would the weekly summary be, if the system were complete?
  3. State one measurement that would tell the board whether a new system is warranted.
Write yours first — 8 minutes
Most probable answer

(a) No — not yet. The arrangement has input (the clipboard), processing (the Sunday typing and totalling) and output (the printed summary on the wall). It has no feedback, because nothing the summary says has ever changed how intake is recorded or how work is scheduled. Under the §1.1.2 definition, feedback is the activity that converts a data-processing routine into a system, so this is a data-processing routine with a wall display attached. Note also the three dimensions of §1.1.3: the board has diagnosed a technology problem ("our data is a mess"), but the observable defect is organisational — no role owns the summary and no procedure requires anyone to act on it. Buying software will not create that role.

(b) The weekly summary is MIS (Management Information System) in shape: a scheduled, routine report comparing recent activity against expectation, aimed at the level that plans work. Its source is a manual TPS (Transaction Processing System) — the intake clipboard. This matters because §1.1.6 says data flows upward: if the condition score is applied inconsistently by different volunteers at intake, every summary above it is confidently wrong, and no purchase fixes that.

(c) A defensible measurement: the number of intake records per month whose frame number is blank, duplicated, or does not match the physical bicycle on a spot audit of twenty units. That is falsifiable, cheap, and tests the actual hypothesis. If the error rate is low, the data is not a mess and the board has a feedback problem, not a data problem.

What loses marks

Answering "yes, it's an information system because they use a spreadsheet." The word computer is not in the definition and neither is the word spreadsheet. Also lost: recommending a system without naming what would be measured before and after.

Draws onWeek 01, §1.3.5, §1.3.6, §1.2.4, §1.1.5.
The scenario

Two independent musical-instrument shops trade four hundred metres apart. Verdun Sound has traded for nineteen years, takes cash and card, keeps repair histories in a paper ledger, and buys strings and accessories from a single distributor who raised prices eleven percent last quarter without notice. Amplitude opened three years ago. Customers create an account to book repairs; the account stores the instrument's service history, and the shop emails a service reminder at eleven months. Amplitude requires a quote from three distributors before ordering accessories. Both shops sell comparable instruments at comparable prices. Amplitude's margin is measurably higher.

The questions
  1. Score the force pressing hardest on each shop and defend the score with observable evidence.
  2. Name the generic strategy Amplitude is pursuing and the system that serves it.
  3. Give one reason Verdun Sound could copy Amplitude's software next month and still not close the gap.
Write yours first — 8 minutes
Most probable answer

(a) For Verdun Sound the strongest force is bargaining power of suppliers. The observable evidence is direct: a single distributor imposed an eleven percent increase with no notice and no negotiation, which is only possible where the buyer has no alternative source and no price history to argue from. For Amplitude the strongest remaining force is rivalry among existing competitors, and it is materially weaker, because the stored service history and the eleven-month reminder mean a share of its repair revenue arrives without the customer shopping around at all. The measurement that would confirm this is the proportion of repair bookings originating from a reminder email rather than a walk-in, tracked over a full year.

(b) Amplitude is pursuing strengthening customer and supplier intimacy (§1.3.6), served on the customer side by what is functionally a small CRM (Customer Relationship Management) system — accumulated service history that the customer cannot take with them — and on the supplier side by a multi-source quoting practice that turns one relationship into a comparable market and directly attacks supplier bargaining power. Note that the customer-side history is also a switching cost (§1.2.4): leaving Amplitude means starting the instrument's documented history again.

(c) Because the software is not the source of the advantage. §1.1.5: the value comes from complementary assets — the habit of requesting three quotes before every order, the procedure that triggers reminders at eleven months, and a staff incentive to record service notes properly. Verdun Sound can buy the licence in an afternoon and will still not have those, and building them takes quarters, not weeks. This is the same reason §1.3.6 warns that buying a system does not produce a strategy.

What loses marks

Scoring all five forces as "high" without evidence sentences. In this course a score whose evidence cell is empty counts as zero, deliberately.

Draws onWeek 02, §1.1, §1.2, §1.4, §1.5, §1.6.
The scenario

A nine-person print and design studio runs its job-tracking software on one physical server in a locked utility room. The server is six years old and out of warranty. The studio has no systems administrator; the owner's nephew, a part-time contractor, handles everything and is available two evenings a week. Backups run to a second disk in the same room. A vendor has proposed three options: rent virtual machines and move the existing software unchanged; rebuild the application on a managed platform; or drop the software entirely for a subscription product built for print shops.

The questions
  1. Map the three proposals to the service models and state who holds the operating system in each.
  2. Which infrastructure components (§1.2) are currently weakest?
  3. Recommend one option and name the cost the proposal will not mention.
Write yours first — 8 minutes
Most probable answer

(a) Proposal one is IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) — the provider holds virtualization, servers, storage, networking and the building; the studio still holds the operating system, runtime, application and data. Proposal two is PaaS (Platform as a Service) — the provider now holds the operating system and runtime; the studio holds application code and data. Proposal three is SaaS (Software as a Service) — the provider holds everything up to and including the application; the studio holds only the data, which it holds in all three, because the Data row of the §1.5 table never moves.

(b) Two components are weak and one is critical. Data management and storage (component 4) is failing: the only backup is a second disk in the same room, so a fire, a theft or a ransomware event that reaches the file share destroys both copies simultaneously — the copy is not offline, so it is not a backup in the §4.4 sense. Consulting and system integration services (component 7) is the critical weakness: one part-time contractor available two evenings a week is the sole holder of operational knowledge for a nine-person business. That is a genuine infrastructure finding even though it is a person, and it should appear in the assessment ahead of the hardware age.

(c) SaaS is the defensible recommendation, precisely because of the component-7 finding: the model whose split of responsibility matches the skills actually on staff is the one that leaves the fewest layers with the studio, and this studio has no administrator. The unmentioned cost is lock-in (§1.6, question three): once three years of job history and customer records sit in the vendor's product, the price of leaving includes export format, re-keying, retraining and the possibility that history simply does not come out in a usable shape. The correct management response is not to reject SaaS but to make a tested data export part of the purchase decision — and to run that export once before signing, not after.

What loses marks

Recommending IaaS because it gives "more control." Control the studio cannot staff is not an asset; it is unpaid work waiting to be skipped.

The scenario

A community choir tracks members, subscriptions and borrowed sheet-music folders in a single spreadsheet with one row per loan. Each row repeats the member's name, phone number, email and voice part alongside the folder code and the date taken. A member who has borrowed nine folders appears in nine rows. The treasurer reports that the choir "keeps phoning wrong numbers" and that two people believe they returned folder SATB-114, which the sheet still shows as out.

The questions
  1. Name the diseases present and the structural cause.
  2. Propose a minimum table design with keys.
  3. Which single database rule would have prevented the SATB-114 dispute?
Write yours first — 8 minutes
Most probable answer

(a) At least three of the five §2.1 diseases are present, and they are causally linked. Data redundancy is the structural cause: the phone number is stored once per loan rather than once per member, so a member with nine loans has nine copies of it. Data inconsistency is the guaranteed consequence — when a number changes, whoever edits the sheet updates the rows they can see and misses the rest, which is exactly the "wrong numbers" symptom. Poor security and sharing is present too: the file is the access control, so anyone who can open it can read every member's contact details, and there is no way to grant the librarian folder access without also granting contact access. The deeper point from §2.2 is that a spreadsheet has no opinion — it stores what was typed, where a database stores only what is allowed.

(b) Three tables. MEMBER — member_id (surrogate key, auto-incrementing, no business meaning), name, phone, email, voice_part. FOLDER — folder_code as primary key, title, part_count. Each fact now lives in exactly one place, which is the whole of normalisation (§2.3): change a phone number once and it is changed everywhere, because there is only one place it exists. Use a surrogate key for the member rather than the email address, for the §2.4 reason — an email address changes, and a primary key value that changes must be updated simultaneously in every table that references it.

(c) Referential integrity on folder_code prevents a loan referencing a folder that does not exist, but the SATB-114 dispute is a state problem rather than a reference problem: two members each believe they returned it. The rule that prevents it is a uniqueness constraint ensuring at most one open loan per folder — a folder with date_returned empty cannot be lent again. The database refuses the second loan at the moment of entry rather than producing a dispute six weeks later. Underneath both answers sits §2.7: someone must own the definition of "returned" and write it down, because a folder handed to a section leader in the car park is either returned or it is not, and the system cannot decide that for you.

Answering "they should use a database" without naming the keys or the constraint. The examinable content is which rule, not which product.

Most probable answer

Recommending a faster internet plan. The measurement in the scenario was placed there specifically to make that answer indefensible.

The scenario

  2. Calculate ALE before and after and give the ROSI.

Most probable answer

(a) The asset is the institution's funds and the integrity of its supplier payment records. Recommending "more security awareness training" alone. Training is a real control, but the arithmetic in the scenario supports a specific procedural change with a calculable return, and a recommendation without the arithmetic is an opinion.

The scenario

A bakery group runs five locations. Each shop keeps its own stock sheet; head office keeps a separate sales spreadsheet; the accountant keeps a third file with prices. On a typical Saturday one shop runs out of a signature loaf by 10:00 while another discards eleven unsold. A regular customer's loyalty card, issued at shop 2, is not recognised at shop 4. Month-end closing takes two weeks and the three files never fully agree. The owner is considering a cloud ERP with a CRM module and asks whether it will "fix the numbers."

  2. Explain, step by step, what changes for one loaf sold at shop 4 after the ERP.
  3. State the honest cost and one measurement of success.
Write yours first — 8 minutes
Most probable answer

(a) The condition is information silos (§1.2): three systems each holding a private copy of overlapping data with no shared record. It is not a staff problem and not a software-quality problem — each file is individually accurate at the moment it is written. It is a structural consequence of each function optimising its own work without a shared record, and the guaranteed outcome is that the copies drift apart. Note the Week 01 §1.2.1 framing as well: the customer experiences one process — order-to-cash — that crosses shop, head office and accounting, and the crossings are exactly where the information is lost.

One real-world event, one entry, every function updated — which is what makes every report agree with itself. Both are falsifiable, both were bad before, and neither requires anyone's opinion. Answering that the ERP "fixes the numbers." It fixes the structure that produced disagreeing numbers. Draws onWeek 03, §2.1, §2.2, §2.3, §2.4.
The scenario

A small publisher sells a printed 120-page study guide at CAD 24 and the identical content as a downloadable file at CAD 12. Printing, warehousing and shipping cost CAD 9.40 per physical copy. Server and bandwidth cost for a download are estimated at CAD 0.03. The publisher currently sells through a national retail chain that takes 40 percent, and is considering selling directly from its own site. A competitor has begun changing its download price several times a week.

The questions
  1. Explain, using marginal cost, why the two products need different pricing logic.
  2. Name what the publisher is considering, and the honest caveat.
  3. Identify the competitor's practice and the market property that makes it possible.
Write yours first — 8 minutes
Most probable answer

(a) Marginal cost is the cost of producing and delivering one more unit. For the printed guide it is CAD 9.40 and it recurs on every single copy forever, so price must cover a real per-unit cost and the arithmetic of profit is straightforward. For the download it is CAD 0.03 whether it is copy ten or copy ten million, because the expensive part — writing the guide — was paid once to make the first copy. This same property explains why streaming subscriptions can be cheap and why a perfect unauthorised copy costs the copier almost nothing.

(b) The publisher is considering disintermediation — removing the intermediate layer, here the retail chain taking 40 percent, by selling directly to the final customer. The caveat from §2.2 is that intermediaries do not simply vanish: new ones appear. A direct store still needs a platform, a payment processor and possibly a delivery or fulfilment service, each taking a percentage, and it also transfers work the retailer was doing — discovery, customer service, returns and refunds — onto the publisher. The correct assessment compares the retail 40 percent against the total of new fees plus the internal cost of the work being absorbed, not against zero.

(c) The competitor is using dynamic pricing, and it is possible because online menu costs — the cost of changing your prices, named for reprinting restaurant menus — have collapsed to editing a database field. Two further §2.1 properties amplify the effect: information richness and density, which lets buyers compare many sellers in seconds and so reduces information asymmetry, and ubiquity, which means the comparison happens at any hour. The consequence for the publisher is that search costs have collapsed for its customers too, so a static price is now competing against a price that moves — and the response is a deliberate pricing policy, not a one-time decision.

Treating the CAD 12 download as "half price" and stopping there. The examinable content is why the cost structures differ, not the arithmetic of the discount.

A student writes: "The parking system is an information system because it uses a computer." Explain what is wrong with this reasoning and state what would actually settle the question.
CoversWeek 01, Lesson 1.1, §1.1.2 — What an information system actually is
HintThe course definition contains four activities and does not contain one particular noun.
Model answer

The reasoning fails because the definition used in this course does not mention computers at all. An information system is a set of related components that collect raw facts, transform them, and deliver a result to people who act on it, together with the feedback that corrects the collection next time. A hospital ward with a paper drug chart, a nurse who reads it, a doctor who signs it and a review meeting that redesigns the chart is a functioning information system; a computer that records and prints without anything changing as a result is not. What settles the question is whether all four activities are present, and the one to test is feedback — has any output ever changed how input is captured? For the parking system, the falsifiable test is specific: name one change to barrier operation, pricing or opening hours that was made because of a report the system produced, and identify who made it. The limitation of this answer is that feedback can exist informally, so an absence of documented change is evidence of a weak loop rather than proof of none.

Why this earns the marksIt names the concept, defines it without the disputed noun, applies a test with a named actor, and states what could disconfirm it.

E2
Two competing firms buy identical software in the same month. One improves; the other does not. Explain the phenomenon and what an assessor should therefore ask about any proposed system.
CoversWeek 01, Lesson 1.1, §1.1.5 — Complementary assets
HintThe concept has three categories and the software is in none of them.
Model answer

The phenomenon is explained by complementary assets — the additional organisational, managerial and social investments an organisation must make before a technology investment produces any value. Organisational assets include a supportive culture, an efficient business process for the system to run inside, and decision authority located where the information arrives. Managerial assets include senior backing, training, and incentives that reward using the system rather than working around it. Social assets include telecommunications infrastructure, a labour market with the relevant skills, standards and enforceable law. The firm that improved had them; the firm that did not, did not — which is why the course states that the software is rarely the expensive part and almost never the part that fails. An assessor should therefore ask, for any proposal, which complementary assets does this system assume, and does the organisation actually have them? The measurement is concrete: name the process step being redesigned, the training hours budgeted, and the incentive that changes. The limitation is that complementary assets are slower to observe than software, so a short assessment window can mistake a lagging benefit for an absent one.

Why this earns the marksThree named categories, an assessment question the reader can reuse, a measurement, and an honest limitation.

E3
Explain why a till operator who rings unusual items through as "miscellaneous" is a strategic problem and not merely an operational annoyance.
CoversWeek 01, Lesson 1.1, §1.1.6 — Four system types, four kinds of question
HintFollow the record upward through the four system types.
Model answer

The till is a TPS (Transaction Processing System), and in the four-type model data flows upward: the TPS feeds the MIS (Management Information System), which reports last month against plan, and both feed the DSS (Decision Support System) and ESS (Executive Support System) that model alternatives and set direction. A miscoded transaction is therefore not confined to one receipt — it propagates into every layer above, which then produces confident, well-formatted, wrong answers. Category mix, reorder rules, supplier negotiations and range decisions all rest on that record. This is why the course states that data quality problems are almost always input problems, and why an assessment should examine the capture step before doubting the report. The measurement is direct: sample two hundred transactions against physical receipts and count the proportion coded to a generic category, then repeat monthly. The limitation of this answer is that miscoding is often a rational response to a slow interface, so the fix may be a design change rather than a discipline change — and a course in management of information systems should say so.

Why this earns the marksIt traces the mechanism upward, names the systems, gives a countable measurement, and resists blaming the operator.

E4
A neighbourhood bookshop buys all its stock from one wholesaler, who raised prices twice last year without notice. Apply the relevant competitive force and recommend one system, with the measurement that would show it worked.
CoversWeek 01, Lesson 1.3, §1.3.5 and §1.3.6 — The five competitive forces; four generic strategies
HintA strong force means value is leaving the firm through that channel.
Model answer

The force pressing hardest is the bargaining power of suppliers, which rises when there are few sources and the buyer has no price history to argue from. The observable evidence is in the scenario: two unilateral increases with no notice and no negotiation, which is only possible where the buyer has no alternative. The recognised systems response is a supplier portal or multi-source quoting arrangement holding price history across several wholesalers, which converts a single relationship into a comparable market — and that supports the generic strategy of strengthening supplier intimacy while simultaneously reducing dependence. The measurement is the average unit cost of the twenty highest-volume titles, tracked monthly for two quarters before and after, alongside the number of orders placed after receiving three or more quotes. Two limitations should be stated. First, a very small shop may lack the volume to interest a second wholesaler, in which case the correct finding is that the force cannot be reduced by systems alone. Second, this recommendation does nothing about rivalry or substitutes, and an assessment that scores only the force it can solve is not an assessment.

Why this earns the marksEvidence for the score, a named mechanism, a falsifiable measurement, and two honest limits.

E5
An automated system suspends a customer account. Nobody in the organisation can explain which rule fired or reverse the decision. Analyse this in the vocabulary of responsibility, accountability and liability, and state the design rule that applies.
CoversWeek 01, Lesson 1.4, §1.4.4 — Responsibility, accountability and liability
HintTwo of the three terms describe things a person feels; one describes a property of a system.
Model answer

Responsibility is a person accepting the potential costs and duties of a decision; liability is a legal framework permitting recovery of damages from the party at fault; accountability is different in kind, because it is the existence of a mechanism that determines who took an action. It is a property of a system, not of a person — so where no such mechanism exists, nobody is accountable regardless of who feels responsible. What the scenario describes is therefore an accountability gap: the action occurred, a customer was harmed, and no mechanism can identify which human decision produced it. Saying "the algorithm did it" describes the gap rather than explaining it. The design rule this course applies is unambiguous: every automatic decision must have a named human who can reverse it, and a system that suspends, refuses or flags with no such person has a defect regardless of its accuracy. The measurable remedy is an audit record that stores, for each automated action, the rule version applied, the inputs used and the reviewer who can override, with the median time from customer challenge to human decision reported monthly. The limitation is that logging identifies what fired without necessarily explaining why the rule was written, so the audit record must be paired with a change history for the rules themselves.

Why this earns the marksPrecise distinctions, correct identification of the gap, the course rule, a measurement, and a real limitation of logging.

E6
A university proposes to enable location analytics for all campus applications, with students able to switch it off in settings. Evaluate this design choice ethically.
CoversWeek 01, Lesson 1.4, §1.4.3, §1.4.5, §1.4.8 — Trends; the five-step analysis; opt-in versus opt-out
HintThe examinable point is not whether tracking is permitted but who chose the default.
Model answer

The proposal is an opt-out design: collection happens unless the student objects. Because the default determines the outcome for the large majority of people, choosing that default is an ethical decision rather than a user-interface decision, and it should be recorded as one. Two of the five moral dimensions are engaged — information rights and obligations, and quality of life — and the mechanism of concern is profiling: location records combined with timetable and network association data describe where a named individual was, for how long, and plausibly with whom, even though no single contributing record is sensitive. Working the five-step analysis briefly: the facts are that collection is enabled by default across all applications; the conflict is service improvement against informational self-determination; the stakeholders include students who never open the settings screen, staff, and a future administrator who inherits the data; the options include opt-in, opt-out, aggregate-only collection, and collecting nothing; and the consequences differ sharply for the student who does not know the setting exists. Informed consent requires knowledge of the facts, so a default buried in a settings menu is agreement without knowledge. The defensible recommendation is opt-in for individual-level location data with aggregate counts collected by default, and the measurement is the proportion of students who actively change the setting in the first term — if it is near zero under opt-out, the consent was nominal. In Québec the applicable authority is the Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec — https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca, and the federal reference is the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada — https://www.priv.gc.ca. The limitation of this answer is that opt-in reduces data volume, which may genuinely degrade a service some students value, and an honest assessment names that cost rather than assuming it away.

Why this earns the marksIt works the framework rather than reciting it, names a specific stakeholder not in the room, gives a measurement of consent quality, and concedes a real cost.

E7
An organisation's SaaS (Software as a Service) provider is breached. The chief executive asks why the organisation, rather than the provider, must notify customers. Explain.
CoversWeek 02, Lesson 1, §1.5 — The three service models
HintOne row of the responsibility table is identical in all four columns.
Model answer

The service models differ only in who holds each layer of the stack. Moving from on-premises to IaaS transfers the building, servers, storage, networking and virtualization to the provider; PaaS adds the operating system and runtime; SaaS adds the application itself. But the Data row never moves. In every model, including SaaS, the data belongs to the organisation that collected it, which is why the organisation remains the party that must notify affected individuals, answer to the regulator and absorb the reputational damage. The compact form is: you can rent the machines, you cannot rent away responsibility for the data. The management consequence is that a SaaS purchase must be assessed for the provider's security posture, breach-notification timelines and evidence obligations as carefully as an internal system, and the measurable control is a contractual notification window with a tested escalation path, exercised at least annually. The limitation is that contracts can allocate cost and cooperation between the parties even though they cannot allocate away the underlying obligation, so a full answer distinguishes the legal duty from the commercial settlement.

Why this earns the marksIt uses the table as an argument rather than reproducing it, and separates duty from cost.

E8
A department insists its shared spreadsheet is "basically a database." Explain the difference and the design principle that resolves the underlying problem.
CoversWeek 02, Lesson 2, §2.2 and §2.3 — What a database actually is; the relational model
HintOne of the two has no opinion about what you type.
Model answer

The difference is enforcement. A spreadsheet stores what you typed; a database stores only what is allowed. A spreadsheet will accept "Montréal", "MTL" and "montreal " with a trailing space in the same column because it has no opinion, whereas a database can be told the value must come from a defined list and will refuse anything else — not warn, not highlight, refuse. That capability is what makes a DBMS (Database Management System) something other than a large grid. The design principle that resolves the department's real problem is normalisation, stated bluntly: every fact is stored in exactly one place, and if you must change something in two places to keep it true, the design is wrong. In practice that means splitting the sheet into entities with a primary key each and joining them with foreign keys protected by referential integrity, so a record cannot reference something that does not exist. The measurement is the count of contradictory duplicate values for the same entity — for example, distinct phone numbers recorded for one person — sampled before and after. The limitation is that normalisation does not repair data already entered wrongly and does not decide definitions; someone must still own what "correct" means for each field, which is a governance task rather than a technical one.

Why this earns the marksIt gives the difference as a mechanism, the principle in one sentence, a counting measurement, and the governance limit.

E9
A business intelligence programme has run for two years and managers still do not trust the numbers. Explain where to look first and why.
CoversWeek 02, Lesson 2, §2.6 and §2.7 — The BI pipeline; data governance
HintOnly one of the five characteristic failures is really technical.
Model answer

Look at definitions, not at the tool. The pipeline has five stages — source systems, extract-transform-load, data warehouse, analysis and reporting, and decision — and each has a characteristic failure: source systems record what is convenient rather than what is needed; transformation rules go undocumented so nobody can reproduce a number; the warehouse goes stale; the dashboard answers a question nobody asked; and the final number is quietly ignored because it contradicts a manager's intuition. Only the stale warehouse is genuinely technical. The remaining four are failures of definition and ownership, which is why the first place to look is data governance: who is accountable for each field being right, what it may be used for, how long it is kept, and what "correct" means for it. The measurable diagnostic is to take three headline figures and ask two analysts independently to reproduce them from source; the proportion that reconcile to the decimal is the trust score, and it is usually the number that ends the argument. The limitation is that governance findings implicate people and are therefore slower to act on than a tool replacement, which is exactly why organisations prefer buying a new dashboard.

Why this earns the marksCorrect diagnosis of category, a reproducible test, and an honest note about why the wrong answer is popular.

E10
Explain why layering, rather than any particular protocol, is described in this course as the real invention of networking.
CoversWeek 02, Lesson 3, §3.2 — The four layers
HintAsk what would have to happen to add a new transmission medium in a non-layered design.
Model answer

In the TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol) model, each layer solves one problem and speaks only to the layers directly above and below it. The application layer does not know whether its data will travel over fibre, copper, radio or satellite; the link layer does not know whether it is carrying an email or a video call. The consequence is substitutability: an entire layer can be replaced without touching any other layer. Wi-Fi was added to the world's networks without rewriting a single web browser, which would have been impossible in a design where every component knew about every other. Encapsulation is the mechanism by which this works — each layer wraps the data in its own header on the way down and the receiver unwraps in reverse — but the architectural property is the invention, and it is why a phone designed this year can talk to software designed in the 1980s over infrastructure owned by companies that never contracted with each other. The measurable consequence for an assessment is that a change confined to one layer should require no regression testing above it, and the number of cross-layer dependencies discovered during a change is a fair proxy for architectural health. The limitation is that real implementations leak across layers — performance tuning and security inspection routinely violate the abstraction — so layering is a design discipline that decays without maintenance rather than a guarantee.

Why this earns the marksIt explains the property rather than listing the layers, gives a concrete historical consequence, and admits that the abstraction leaks.

E11
A manager asks you to recommend "the best security controls." Explain why this request is malformed, and describe how you would answer it defensibly.
CoversWeek 02, Lesson 4, §4.1, §4.2, §4.5 — What security protects; risk vocabulary; the arithmetic
HintAn analyst who recommends every control is providing no information.
Model answer

The request is malformed because security is not a product but a management discipline for deciding how much loss you are willing to accept, and then spending accordingly. There is no configuration that maximises confidentiality, integrity and availability simultaneously, since the most confidential system is one nobody can access and the most available has no login at all — so every real decision is a chosen position on that trade-off. A defensible answer starts from the risk vocabulary: name the asset, the threat (which cannot be removed), the vulnerability (which can), and the proposed control. It then converts the argument into arithmetic: SLE (Single Loss Expectancy) × ARO (Annualized Rate of Occurrence) = ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy), with risk reduction as the difference in ALE before and after the control and ROSI (Return on Security Investment) as that reduction less the control's annual cost, divided by the cost. Where the reduction is smaller than the cost, the recommendation is to decline — and being able to decline two controls is what makes a recommendation of the third credible. Two limitations must be stated in any professional answer: the inputs, especially ARO, are estimates rather than measurements, so the value of the arithmetic is that it makes assumptions visible and arguable; and the model handles frequent moderate losses well and rare catastrophic ones badly, so for an event with a small chance of ending the organisation you buy insurance or avoid the activity rather than optimising expected value.

Why this earns the marksIt reframes the question, supplies a method rather than a shopping list, and names precisely where the method stops applying.

E12
A five-branch retailer asks whether an ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) system will "fix our numbers." Answer the question honestly.
CoversWeek 03, Lesson 1, §1.2, §1.3, §1.6; Week 03, Lesson 4, §4.4
HintThe system fixes a structure; it does not fix what is typed into it.
Model answer

An ERP does not fix the numbers; it removes the structure that produced disagreeing numbers. The present condition is information silos — separate systems each holding a private copy of overlapping data — and the drift between copies is structural rather than a staff failing, because there is no shared record for any department to be right about. An ERP replaces those with one shared central database, so a single sale drops the inventory count, records the revenue once at the current official price and updates the customer record in the same instant. That is what makes every report agree with itself, and that agreement — the single source of truth — is a precondition for the branch-to-branch transfer rule and the month-end close. But the honest half is that the ERP will faithfully propagate whatever it is given: if staff record unusual items as "miscellaneous" because it is faster, the group now has consistent, confident, wrong figures, which is GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out) arriving on schedule. The measurements to commit to before purchase are days from period end to closed books, currently fourteen, and unsold units discarded per week per branch. The limitations to state plainly: enterprise applications are expensive in licences, consultants, training and months of work; they force the business to change habits to match the software's built-in processes, which people resist; and they create switching costs against the buyer, so leaving the vendor later will be expensive. Installing the software is the easy half.

Why this earns the marksIt answers the actual question, gives the mechanism, commits to two measurements, and refuses to sell the easy half.

E13
A publisher sells the same guide in print and as a download. Explain why the two products require different pricing logic.
CoversWeek 03, Lesson 2, §2.3 — Digital goods
HintThe term names the cost of one more unit.
Model answer

The difference is marginal cost — the cost of producing and delivering one additional unit. For the printed guide, marginal cost is real money on every copy, forever: paper, printing, warehousing and shipping. For the identical content delivered as a file, marginal cost is a few cents of server and bandwidth, and it does not change whether the sale is the tenth or the ten-millionth. Making the first copy can be equally expensive in both cases, because writing the guide is the same work; what differs is everything after that. When marginal cost is effectively zero, price is no longer determined by cost, so pricing the digital edition becomes a strategy question — a choice between volume, positioning and perceived value — rather than a cost calculation with a margin added. This single property explains why streaming subscriptions can be inexpensive, why software firms scale so quickly, and why unauthorised copying is such a persistent fight: a perfect copy costs the copier almost nothing. The measurement that would test a chosen price is contribution per title per month at two price points held for a full quarter each. The limitation is that a very low digital price can devalue the printed edition in customers' eyes, so the two prices are not independent decisions even though their cost structures are.

Why this earns the marksIt defines the term precisely, draws the consequence for pricing, and notices that the two products interact.

E14
The most experienced technician on a help desk retires in four months. Explain the risk in the course vocabulary and the first step to take.
CoversWeek 03, Lesson 3, §3.2 and §3.3 — Tacit versus explicit; the KM value chain
HintName the property before you name the process.
Model answer

The risk is the loss of tacit knowledge — knowledge that lives in a person's head and hands, learned by experience, hard to write down completely, and possessing the critical property that it walks out of the building whenever its owner does. The technician who identifies a failing bearing from the sound of a fan holds exactly this. Explicit knowledge, by contrast, has been written into a transferable form — procedures, checklists, well-commented code — and survives its author. The first step of the knowledge management value chain is acquire: capture what the expert explains, the solved case, and the decision together with its reasons, which is the step organisations skip when they are "too busy", meaning always. Storing, sharing and applying follow, and applying is what reveals the remaining gaps and restarts the cycle. Because acquisition costs the expert's time, the assessment question is the one the course insists on: who is given time to contribute, and when? If the answer is nobody, the knowledge base will be empty within a year. The measurable target here is specific: a documented diagnostic procedure for the twenty most frequent ticket categories, with a second technician resolving each unaided as the acceptance test. The limitation is that some tacit knowledge genuinely does not reduce to text — pattern recognition acquired over years — so paired working and recorded walkthroughs should supplement written capture rather than being replaced by it.

Why this earns the marksCorrect property, correct first step, the funding question, an acceptance test, and an admission that not all tacit knowledge converts.

E15
A bank automates loan pre-approval using historical decisions as training data. State the two warnings from Week 03 that apply and the professional obligation that follows.
CoversWeek 03, Lesson 4, §4.4; Lesson 3, §3.4 — Two warnings; the AI rule
HintOne warning is about the data going in; the other is about what the data encodes.
Model answer

The first warning is GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out): output quality can never exceed input quality, so a system trained on incomplete or inconsistently recorded histories will produce confident decisions built on defects nobody sees, and a well-designed interface makes those decisions look more authoritative rather than less. The second warning is sharper, because the data may be complete and still wrong to learn from: when structured decisions are fully automated, the rules and the historical data they learned from can contain bias, and the system will then repeat that bias tirelessly and at scale — faster and more consistently than the humans whose decisions it copied. The professional obligation that follows is to treat automated decisions as something to monitor and audit, not to set and forget, and it connects directly to the course's rule on intelligent techniques: the model drafts, the analyst decides, the analyst's name goes on the record. Whoever deploys the system owns its mistakes. Concretely, that means periodic outcome review by applicant segment, a documented override path with a named reviewer, and retention of the rule version applied to each decision. The measurement is the approval rate and the subsequent default rate by segment, reviewed quarterly against the pre-automation baseline. The limitation is that measuring for bias requires collecting the very attributes an organisation may be reluctant or legally constrained to record, so the monitoring design is itself a governance and privacy question rather than a purely statistical one.

Why this earns the marksBoth warnings named and distinguished, the ownership rule quoted, a monitoring design, and the honest tension in measuring it.

E16
A requirements document contains the line "the system must be user friendly." Explain why this is unacceptable and rewrite it.
CoversWeek 04, Lesson 1, §3.3 — Systems analysis
HintA requirement earns its place by being capable of failing a test.
Model answer

The line is unacceptable because it is not testable: different readers interpret it differently, so there is no state of the world in which it is demonstrably unmet, and a requirement that cannot fail cannot be verified at acceptance. Requirements describe a capability, rule, constraint, quality expectation or control the system must satisfy, and they must be written so that a test can be constructed against them. A defensible rewrite names the user, the task and the observable result: "A first-time student, having read the one-page instruction and without staff assistance, can search the catalogue, select an available item and submit a reservation; at least eight of ten first-time participants complete this in under three minutes during acceptance testing." That version can be put in front of real people and can fail. It also belongs to a priority — Must, Should, Could, or not in this release — because the purpose of prioritisation is not to make features disappear but to force an explicit decision about what the first usable release requires. The measurement is embedded in the requirement itself, which is the point. The limitation is that a task-completion threshold measures learnability for one path only; a system can pass this test and remain unusable on a path nobody wrote a requirement for, so the technique reduces ambiguity rather than guaranteeing quality.

Why this earns the marksIt gives the reason, produces an actually testable rewrite with a threshold, and notes what the rewrite does not cover.

E17
A college outsources development and operation of its student portal. The vice-principal states that "the vendor is now accountable for the portal." Assess that statement.
CoversWeek 04, Lesson 1, §3.6; Week 01, Lesson 1.4, §1.4.4
HintOutsourcing transfers work; it does not transfer a particular thing.
Model answer

The statement is wrong in the sense that matters. Outsourcing transfers delivery work and can supply skills and capacity the organisation lacks, but it does not transfer accountability for business outcomes, data governance, contract management or the ability to operate the system after delivery. If the portal fails during registration, the college's students are affected, the college's regulator asks the questions, and the college's reputation absorbs the damage — and if personal data is mishandled, the obligations under PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act) and Québec's regime remain with the institution that collected it. What a contract can do is allocate cost, define service levels and specify cooperation; what it cannot do is create an accountability mechanism where none exists internally. The management consequence is that knowledge transfer and exit arrangements should be treated as requirements rather than optional contract language, alongside a named internal owner who can make decisions about the service. The measurable controls are a documented exit plan with a tested data export, a named college-side service owner, and the median time from incident report to college-side decision. The limitation is that a college with no internal capability at all cannot exercise these controls no matter what the contract says, which is why capability retention is itself a requirement — and often the one quietly dropped to reduce cost.

Why this earns the marksIt corrects the claim precisely, distinguishes contract from accountability, and identifies the requirement most likely to be cut.

E18
A sponsor complains that change control is "bureaucracy that slows us down." Respond.
CoversWeek 04, Lesson 2, §4.7 — Change control
HintState what change control is for before defending it.
Model answer

Change control is not designed to prevent change; it is designed to prevent invisible consequences. A requested change may be individually sensible — adding QR (Quick Response) code check-in to a conference portal probably improves the event — and the correct response is neither to reject it because it was not in the plan nor to accept it because it sounds useful. The correct response is impact analysis: which requirements change, which design changes, which code changes, which test cases are added, what security or privacy effects arise, which schedule dependencies move, what it costs, and whether another feature should be removed to protect the date. That analysis is then presented to the person with authority to approve, reject, defer or exchange scope, which is normally the sponsor rather than the project manager. Far from slowing delivery, this is what makes speed safe, because the alternative is a change accepted verbally in a corridor whose effect on the critical path is discovered during acceptance testing. In iterative delivery the process can be lighter for uncommitted backlog items and stricter for anything touching an active iteration, a contractual baseline, architecture, security or the release date — "agile" does not mean scope, cost and risk stop requiring decisions. The measurement is the proportion of approved changes whose actual schedule effect fell within the analysed estimate. The limitation is that heavy process on trivial changes genuinely does waste time, so the threshold at which full analysis is triggered should itself be a documented decision.

Why this earns the marksIt concedes the sponsor's real point, states the purpose, names the decision authority, and proposes a threshold.

E19
A platform hosted in Québec is opening a support desk in Spain. Session timestamps are currently stored in local time. Explain the two operational problems and the correct design.
CoversWeek 04, Lesson 3, §5.9 — Time zones and support operations
HintOne problem concerns the data; the other concerns whether the service is ready at all.
Model answer

The first problem is that timestamps stored in local time cannot be reconciled across regions. Time zones affect monitoring, release coordination, incident response, batch processing, report cutoffs, deadlines and the meaning of every recorded event, and daylight-saving transitions make fixed offsets unreliable for production logic. The correct design is to store precise timestamps in UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) and convert for display according to user or business context, using a recognised time-zone database rather than manually entered offsets — while accepting that local legal or operational records may still require local time with zone information recorded alongside. The second problem is operational readiness: support can be centralized, regional or follow-the-sun, where responsibility passes between teams as their working days begin and end. Follow-the-sun gives long coverage without one team working continuously, but it requires disciplined handoff records, shared ticket data, escalation rules and consistent procedures — and the course states the standard plainly: a global system with no practical support during a region's working day is not operationally ready even if every technical test passed. The measurement is the proportion of tickets raised in each region that receive a first substantive response within the target, reported by region rather than as a global average, since an average conceals exactly the regional gap. The limitation is that converting existing local-time records to UTC requires knowing which zone each was written in, and where that was never recorded the historical data may be irrecoverably ambiguous — which is an argument for correcting the design immediately rather than after the Spanish launch.

Why this earns the marksTwo distinct problems, the correct storage design with its exception, the readiness standard, a per-region measurement, and a migration limitation.

E20
A region scores 4.5 out of 5 on the rollout readiness scorecard. The missing half point is a required regulatory approval. The programme director proposes launching on schedule. Assess.
CoversWeek 04, Lesson 3, §5.11 and §5.5 — Rollout strategy; data protection
HintA weighted score is a management aid, not a substitute for a gate.
Model answer

The region should not launch. Rollout should be based on readiness evidence rather than a calendar, and although readiness scores help compare regions, they must not hide mandatory criteria. A weighted average is designed to let strength in one area offset weakness in another, which is appropriate for support staffing or training completion and entirely inappropriate for a legal precondition — a regulatory approval is a gate, not a weight, and the arithmetic of the scorecard has no authority over it. The correct structure is to define entry criteria before a region joins a wave: completed privacy review, approved localization, successful integration tests, support staffing, completed data migration and signed business readiness, with the mandatory items marked as gates that produce a launch decision of "no" regardless of the composite score. The measurement is simply whether each gate has documented evidence and a named approver, and the reporting should show gates separately from the weighted score so that the two are never confused on a slide. Two limitations are worth stating. Slipping a wave has real costs — contracted staff, marketing commitments, and the credibility of the programme — so the recommendation is to slip this region rather than the whole wave where the design permits regional independence. And a scorecard cannot tell you whether the regulatory item was correctly identified in the first place, which is why the privacy review must involve qualified local advice rather than a checklist completed centrally.

Why this earns the marksIt refuses the launch on principle, distinguishes gates from weights, proposes a reporting change, and prices the cost of its own recommendation.

Twelve things that reliably appear on the paper. If you can answer all twelve out loud without notes, stop revising and sleep.

  • Name the four activities of an information system and say which one beginners omit.
  • Name the six strategic business objectives and give a two-objective claim for a system you use.
  • Explain complementary assets to someone who thinks the software is the investment.
  • Match TPS, MIS, DSS and ESS to their four questions, and explain why data quality is an input problem.
  • State the five competitive forces and the systems response to the one about new entrants.
  • Distinguish responsibility, accountability and liability, then define the accountability gap.
  • Read down the Data row of the cloud responsibility table and say what it means after a provider breach.
  • Give the five diseases of the traditional file environment and the one-sentence rule of normalisation.
  • Compute an ALE and a ROSI from an SLE, an ARO and a control cost — and name the two limitations of the formula.
  • Distinguish tacit and explicit knowledge, then give the four steps of the knowledge management value chain.
  • Find the critical path in a five-task network and say what happens when an off-path task slips.
  • Separate global core from controlled local variation using the two review questions.

Deadline 16 Aug 2026 midnight CET

You will pick one real system you personally use and produce a professional assessment of it — not a description, not a review. The difference is falsifiability: every claim must be precise enough that someone who disagrees with you could go and check.

A description Says what a system is.
A review Says whether you liked it.
An assessment Says what it's for, whether it works, how you measured it, what it costs, what you'd change first.

Plain-Language Toolkit

Every term you need, in ordinary words. Click a category to expand it.

IT (Information Technology) — the machinery: computers, phones, cables, servers, software.

IS (Information System) — the machinery plus the people, rules and habits around it.

Data → Information → Knowledge — raw facts → data organised to answer a question → understanding of why, usually held by a person.

Input → Processing → Output → Feedback — the four activities every information system performs; feedback is the one beginners forget.

Business process — a specific sequence of steps by specific people that produces something somebody wants.

Handoff — the moment work crosses from one person/team/department to another. Almost everything expensive happens at a handoff.

Information silo — two parts of an org keeping separate records of the same facts, which quietly disagree.

Stakeholder — anybody with an interest in the outcome, including people who never touch the system.

Baseline — the measurement taken before changing anything, so later you can prove something moved. Part A is your baseline.

TPSFront-line staff, tills, sensors"Record this sale, this shift, this shipment."
MISMiddle managers"How did we do last month against plan?"

ERP — Enterprise Resource Planning: one shared database across finance, staff, production, sales.
SCM — Supply Chain Management: coordinates suppliers, production, distribution outside company walls.
CRM — Customer Relationship Management: stores every customer interaction.
KMS — Knowledge Management System: captures expertise so it survives an employee leaving.

Competitive force — one of five pressures pushing money out of a business (Porter's Five Forces).

Switching cost — what it would cost a customer to leave. Raising it is both a strategy and an ethical question.

Transaction cost — what it costs to buy from outside rather than do in-house.

Agency cost — what it costs an owner to supervise employees.

Complementary assets — the non-software things (training, incentives, management backing) a system needs to actually work.

Responsibility — a person accepts the costs and duties of a decision.

Accountability — a mechanism exists to determine who took an action. Property of a system, not a person.

Liability — a legal framework lets you recover damages.

Accountability gap — a system acts, someone is harmed, no mechanism can trace it to a human decision.

Profiling — combining harmless-looking records to build a detailed picture of an individual.

Data minimisation — collect only what the stated purpose actually requires.

Informed consent — agreement given with real knowledge of the facts, not buried in fine print.

PIPEDA / Law 25 / GDPR / CCPA — Canadian federal, Québec, EU and California privacy frameworks respectively.

⛔ The Hard Limits

No exceptions, no interpretations. These separate an assignment from a problem.

1. Observe only as an ordinary user.Use the system exactly as any member of the public is entitled to.
2. No security testing of any kind.No scanning, fuzzing, injecting, intercepting — potentially a Criminal Code offence.
3. No accessing anything you weren't given.No other accounts, no admin panels, no unlisted URLs.
4. No scraping or automation.Take notes by hand, like an auditor with a clipboard.
5. Collect no personal data.Use role labels — "the counter clerk", "Student B".
6. No confidential/internal material.Employment obligations always outrank this assignment.
7. Anonymise where in doubt.Pseudonymise small subjects; say that you have.
8. Do not publish.Dossier goes to the DropBox only — nowhere else, ever.
9. If asked to stop, stop.A refused subject costs 15 minutes; pushing past costs far more.
10. When in doubt, ask first.Nobody has ever lost marks for asking in the course forum.

The Five Moral Dimensions

Information rights & obligationsWhat's collected, disclosed, and can a person see/correct/delete it?
Property rights & obligationsWho owns the data and anything derived from it?
Accountability, liability & controlWhen it goes wrong, who answers? Can a human reverse an automatic decision?
System qualityWhat error rate is acceptable, given what an error does to a person?
Quality of lifeWhat does this change about how people behave, wait, queue or relate to each other?

The Five-Step Ethical Analysis

Apply this to the single sharpest ethical question your system raises.

1
The factsWho does what, to whom, when, how — this often dissolves the "dispute".
2
The conflict & higher-order valuesName both legitimate values in tension (e.g. safety vs. privacy).
3
The stakeholdersEverybody with an interest, including someone not in the room.
4
The optionsFind at least four — the good one is usually the third.
5
The consequencesFor each option, for each stakeholder — not just the organisation.

Then apply two of the six candidate principles (Golden Rule, Categorical Imperative, Slippery Slope, Utilitarian, Risk Aversion, "No Free Lunch") that disagree, and say which you'd follow.

✅ What earns marks

  • A specific measurement behind every claim
  • At least one thing the system does not do well
  • An honest "Low confidence" label on an inference
  • Naming a stakeholder nobody else would think of

❌ What loses marks

  • Adjectives standing in for measurements ("seamless", "robust")
  • Claiming all six business objectives
  • Any lens left blank, or a score with no evidence sentence
  • Reusing the fictional lecture case studies as your subject

Six-Lens Weighting

Ethics and Evidence carry double weight — that's where real assessments fail.
